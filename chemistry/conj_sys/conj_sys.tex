  \subsection{Conjugated π Systems}

    \begin{core}{Conjugated π System Stability}

      A conjugated pi system refers to network of adjacent allylic stuctures that allow for resonance across a greater number of carbons. For instance, take a look at the hydrogenation enthalpies of the following alkenes.

      <5 reactions figure>

      Compound 5 is more stabilized than predicted based on intuitive addition of constituent enthalpies because of the ability of conjugated π systems to better stabilize excess or lacking charge.

      <energy digram figure>

      When we look closer into the orbital energy diagrams of these conjugated π systems like we have done in REFERENCE TO 3 PI SYSTEM, we find that the conjugated π system orbtial energy levels sit in a an overall larger range but possesses smaller stepwise increases in energy, thus the HOMO LUMO gap is comparaitively smaller compared to the non conjugated πsystem alkenes.

      <pi orbtial energy diagram figure>
      caption: source of stability in a conjugated π system are the lowest π MO \ce{e^-}s

      The rules are the following.

      \begin{enumerate}

        \item As onjugation increases (more p orbitals in a row), the energy gap between π MOs get smaller

        \item As conjugation increases, the lowest energy π MO gets lower in energy and the highest energy π MO goes higher in energy

      \end{enumerate}

      It's important to know that every high energy intermediate needs to be as stabilized as possible and that resonance from conjugated π systems trump stability originating from hyperconjugation.
      \[
        \text{resonance} > 3^\circ > 2^\circ > 2^\circ
      \]

      This has particularly large consequences in the different reactions covered within \href{https://ibahng.github.io/chemistry/alkene_alkyne/alkene_alkyne.pdf}{Alkenes \& Alkynes} because many electrophilic additions progress in a Markovnikov or anti-Markovnikov manner, which takes into consideration intermediate state stability. Examples include alkene hydrohalogenation (\textit{\autoref{alk-core:hydrohalogenation}}), which progresses in a Markovnikov manner, and hydroboration-oxidation (\textit{\autoref{alk-core:alkene_hydro_oxi}}), which progresses in an anti-Markovnikov matter.

    \end{core}

  \subsection{Rates of Diel Alder Reactions}

    \begin{core}{Diene Orientation}

      Diel alder reactions are more favorable when the resulting ring has minimal strain. The \textbf{s-cis} diene orientation does this better than the \textbf{s-trans} orientation as shown below. \textit{The s in s-cis and s-trans stands for specifically along single bond as clarification because conventionally cis and trans in the context of alkenes look at the double bond as the reference bond.}

      <figure of s-cis and s-trans>
      caption: a ring needs to be at least 8 atoms large to accommodate a trans π bond

      Take the following dienes for instance.

      <figure> 

      The relative diel alder reaction rates is $\text{C} > \text{A} > \text{B}$. C reigns superior because its structure restricts rotation along the relevant single bond, preventing any rearrangement to an s-trans arrangement. A and B, on the other hand, both can rotate along the relevant single bond, however B favors the s-trans arrangement because the s-cis arangement has a comparatively higher level of steric hinderance between the alkenyl substituents to the relevant single bond.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Secondary Orbital Overlap}

      Under certain scenarios, orbitials not directly responsible for new bond formation between the diene and dienophile can undergo a secondary overlap to lower the overall E$_\text{a}$. Take a look at the following diel alder interaction between two cyclopentenes.

      <figure>

      It's important to keep in mind that although favorable mixing of secondary orbital overlaps in the transition state will help stabilize that transition state, sterics still does not favor the endo approach. As a result, ambigiuous diene dienophile pairs will usually need to undergo experiments to see which effect is more impartant in each case.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Electronic Effects}

      Diel alder reactions progress faster when the energies of the interacting orbitals are closer to one another. In other words, the small the HOMO-LUMO gap between reacting molecules, the faster the rate. This usually occurs when more conjugation occurs.

      However, while more conjugation typically shrinks the HOMO-LUMO gap and speeds up a reaction, it also alters the wave topology (\textit{the layout of the nodes}). If the orbital with the ideal energy gap happens to place a node on an essential bonding atom, that orbital becomes useless for the reaction, forcing the system to use a higher-energy, slower pathway. Symmetry and overlap must always be satisfied before energy gaps even matter.

      <figure>
      caption: The left reagents actually go faster despite the small π3-π$^*$ gap of the right reagents because π3 is not favorable because there is a node of the atom that needs ot make a new σ bond

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Groundstate Destabilization}

      Given that two sets of reagents that are similar will have similar transition states leading to the \textbf{same} product, the reactant pair in which the ground states of the initial diene and dienophile start at a higher energy levels (\textit{due to something such as steric hinderance}) will have a faster diel alder reaction rate. Take the following diene dineophile pairs leading to the same product for instance.

      <figure>

      The energy level for the right diene dineophile pair possesses a greater level of steric hinderance between the isobutyl substituents wihtin the diene, placing it at a higher energy level relative to the left diene dienophile pair.

      <figure>

      As shown in the energy diagram above, the E$_\text{a}$ is much lower for the more unstable reactant pair and thus progresses at a much faster rate because the total ΔH is lower.

    \end{core}

        \textbf{Inductive O/P directors} are substituents that direct any EAS to the O and P positions within an aromatic ring.

      \begin{core}{Inductive M Directors}

        \textbf{Inductive M directors} are substituents that direct any EAS to the M position within an aromatic ring. The substituents are carbons bearing highly electronegative atoms, making the carbon highly partially positive.

        The M position is favored because the O/P 3$^\circ$ allylic carbocation resonance structures have possess extra unstable repulsion between the carbocation and the partial positive carbon within the substituent.       \begin{core}[label=core:rm_director]{Resonance M Directors}

        \textbf{Resonance M directors} are substituents that direct any EAS to the M position within an aromatic ring through withdrawing \ce{e^-} density. For instance, carbonyl substituents have the ability to withdraw \ce{e^-} density in a manner that results in subsequent resonance structures to place a carbocation positive charge on the O/P positions, making these locations effectively less nucleophilic and the P psoition relatively more nucleophilic.

        <figure>

        As a result, when an electrophile approaches the transient aromatic ring, the nucleophilic attack occurs most favorably from the M position rather than the O/P position.

        <figure>

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}{Halogens as Directing Groups}

        Halogens can also act as directing groups, and based on data, they act as O/P directors. To understand the underlying basis of this observation, let's analyze monohalobenzene in the context of the inductive effect and resonance.

        <figure>

        \begin{enumerate}

          \item \textbf{Inductive Effect} 

          <figure>

          Under an O/P directing EAS interaction, inductive analysis would actually discourage O/P directing becaus the subsequent carbocation transition state places a carbocation that already has lowered \ce{e^-} density even lower by residing next to the highly electronegative halogen atom. As a result, the dipole moment created destabilizaes the structure.

          \item \textbf{Resonance}

          <figure>

          Resonance, on the other hand, allows the halogen to donate \ce{e^-} density in order to fill all octet requirements throughout the entire molecule, which favors the O/P directing EAS interaction.

        \end{enumerate}

        Although these two effects contradict one another, we can confidently say that the effects of resonance trump the inductive effect because real data in practice results in the O/P directing as favored.

      \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Nucleophilic Aromatic Substitution}

      \textbf{Nucleophilic aromatic substitution (NAS or S$_\text{N}$Ar)} is an interaction in which the aromatic ring is the electrophile. NAS comes in two "flavors": \textbf{diazonium} (\textit{\autoref{core:diazonium_chem}}) or \textbf{addition/elimination} (\textit{\autoref{core:add_elim}}) chemistry.

      \textit{Recall that in electrophilic aromatic substitution (\autoref{sec:eas}), the aromatic ring is the nucleophile.}

    \end{core}

      Diazonium chemistry begins by \ce{N2} leaving a phenyldiazonium molecule---via breaking the bond between the σ C(sp$^2$)--N(sp) bond---to produce a carbocation possessing benzene ring and an extermely stable \ce{N2} gas moleucle.

      The NAS \textbf{addition/elimination} reaction occurs in the following general manner.

      <figure>

      Recall that we have seen resonance acceptors in resonance M directors (\textit{\autoref{core:rm_director}}), however, the these resonance acceptors are not for the purpose of directing meta in the context of NAS chemistry. Their main purpose is to be able to accept \ce{e^-} density after the nucleophile attack the carbon with the leaving group.

      Additionally, this reaction requires a strong nucleophiles because it ultimately needs to break aromaticity as shown in the mechanism below. It should be noted that this step---the addition---is consequently the rate determining step.

      <figure>

      As the mechanism showcases, the reaction is simply a relay of \ce{e^-} density to the resonance acceptor, which then bounces back the \ce{e^-} density in order to kick out the leaving group in the end. However, since the outcome of this interaction is ultimately the same as that of an \snt reaction, one may wonder why \snt is not a viable pathway.

      Revisiting the \snt mechanism, recall that, in the lense of molecular orbitals, the nucleophile interacts with the σ$^*$ anti-bonding orbital between the electrophile and leaving group in order to kick out the leaving group and create a bond simultaneously via a backside attack.

      <figure>

      In the aromatic ring scenario, this nessecary σ$^*$ interaction is extremely unlikely due to the physcial barrier to entry because of the ring itself.

      In the context of the frontier molecular orbital theory, the π$^*$ orbital is lower in energy than σ$^*$, making it the LUMO and a much more accessible orbital to interact with, which is what happens in addition/elimination, the favorable pathway.
