    \begin{core}{Aromatic Compound Molecular Diagrams}

      The \textbf{Frost Diagram} is a method to quickly predict π molecular orbital energy levels for a cyclic conjugated system, following the following steps.

      \begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]

        \item Compound must have acyclic array of p orbitals 

        \item Draw shape pointed down

        \item Draw a circle around the shape 

        \item Intersections are energy levels

        \item Middle of circle is nonbonding

        \item Add \ce{e^-}s

      \end{enumerate}

    \begin{core}[label=core:aromaticity_rules]{Aromaticity Rules}

      In order to a compound to be considered aromatic, it must follow a strict set of rules.

      \begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]

        \item Cyclic array of p orbitals

        \item \ce{e^-} count in cyclic array fulfills the equation of $4n+2$, where $n$ is an integer (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots).

        \textit{An \ce{e^-} count that fulfills the equation of $4n$ where $n$ is an integer is much more reactive than predicted and is termed \textbf{anti-aromatic}}

      \end{enumerate}

      If the above criteria are not met, the compound is considered \textbf{nonaromatic}, such as the cyclohexene shown below.

    \begin{core}{Aromatic Contributors}

      An \textbf{aromatic contributor} refers to a resonance structure of a molecule that possesses a fully conjugated, cyclic, planar π system satisfying the $4n+2$ π \ce{e^-} count, even if the dominant or lowest-energy Lewis structure doesn't immediately look aromatic. Take the following molecule for instance.
