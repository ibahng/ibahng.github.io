\section{Alkenes}
  \label{sec:alkenes}

  \begin{core}[label=core:alkene_orbital]{Alkene Orbital Structure}

    The orbital structure of an alkene double bond arises from the combination of a σ bond and a π bond. This additional π overlap results in a shorter bond length relative to an alkane single bond: whereas a typical C--C single bond measures roughly $1.5\,A$, the C=C double bond is contracted to approximately $1.3\,A$.

    \myfig{1}{alkene_alkyne_figures/alkene_sigma_pi}{Alkene σ (\textit{left}) and π (\textit{right}) bonding orbitals}{fig:ph1}

  \end{core}

  \begin{core}{Alkene Bond Strength}

    The π bond of an alkene is relatively weaker than its partner σ bond because the antibonding π$^*$ orbital sits closer in energy to the bonding π orbital than the antibonding σ$^*$ orbital sits to the bonding σ orbital. As a result, breaking an alkene π bond requires roughly $65\,kcal\,mol^{-1}$, whereas breaking the σ bond requires $108\,kcal\,mol^{-1}$.

    \mofig{
      \AO[seven](3cm){s}{2.25;}
      \AO[four](3cm){s}{1.5;}
      \AO[five](3cm){s}{0.75}
      \AO[eight](3cm){s}{0}
      \node[right] at (seven.east) {σ$^*$};
      \node[right] at (four.east)  {π$^*$};
      \node[right] at (five.east)  {π};
      \node[right] at (eight.east) {σ};
      \EnergyAxis[title=E]
    }{Molecular bonding orbitals levels of σ, π, π$^*$, and σ$^*$ in an alkene double bond; \textit{notice that the distance between π and π$^*$ is less than that of σ and σ$^*$}}{fig:ph2}{1}

    By comparison, the typical alkane $\sigma$ C--C bond strength is roughly $88\,kcal\,mol^{-1}$. The alkene σ bond is therefore stronger than the alkane σ bond, since the alkene σ bond is formed from $sp^2$ hybridized orbitals rather than the $sp^3$ orbitals used in alkanes. This \textbf{greater s-orbital character}---33\% versus 25\%, respectively---corresponds to greater bond strength, as the bonding \ce{e^-}s are held closer to the nucleus relative to p orbitals.

    Consequently, when comparing the $pK_a$ values of the substituent hydrogens on alkenes and alkanes, alkenes exhibit lower $pK_a$ values than alkanes.

      }
    }{Butene isomer conversions to butane}{fig:ph6}{0.8}

    The greater relative stability of internal double bonds over terminal ones, and of trans over cis substituents, arises from two factors.

    \begin{enumerate}

      \item \textbf{Hyperconjugation} allows the temporary charge imbalance arising in the transition state to be better distributed among neighboring orbitals in the internal bond butene than in its terminal counterpart.

      \item \textbf{Steric hindrance} between the cis methyl groups increases the relative instability of the cis butene compared to its trans counterpart.

      \reactionfig{
        \scheme{
          \chemname{
            \chemfig{
              [:0]C
              (<:[:135]H)
              (<[:-135]\textcolor{Red}{\textbf{\ce{H3C}}})
              =[:0]C
              (<:[:45]H)
              (<[:-45]\textcolor{Red}{\textbf{\ce{CH3}}})
            }
          }{cis}
          \qquad \qquad \qquad
          \chemname{
            \chemfig{
              [:0]C
              (<:[:135]H)
              (<[:-135]\textcolor{Red}{\textbf{\ce{H3C}}})
              =[:0]C
              (<:[:45]\textcolor{Red}{\textbf{\ce{CH3}}})
              (<[:-45]H)
            }
          }{trans}
        }
      }{Steric hindrance between methyl groups of cis (\textit{left}) vs trans (\textit{right}) butene; \textit{more steric hindrance exists between methyl groups of cis butene because of closer methyl group proximity}}{fig:ph8}{0.8}

    \end{enumerate}

    The general order of alkene stability thus follows as shown below.
    \[
      \ce{CH2=CH2} \quad < \quad \ce{RCH=CH2} \quad < \quad \ce{RCH=CHR}_{\text{cis}} \quad < \quad \ce{RCH=CHR}_{\text{trans}} \quad < \quad \ce{RCH=CR2} \quad < \quad \ce{R2C=CR2}
    \]

  \end{core}

  \subsection{Alkene Synthesis}

  \subsection{Alkene Addition Reactions}

    \begin{core}{Alkene Addition Reactions}

      Alkenes serve as fundamental building blocks for organic synthesis and the preparation of polymer materials. Their defining feature, the \textbf{unsaturated π bond}, reacts through \textbf{addition reactions}, in which the π bond is broken and a diatomic or compound molecule is added across the relevant carbons of the former alkene.

      \begin{core}[label=core:alkene_oxymercuration]{Oxymercuration--Demercuration}

        \textbf{Oxymercuration--demercuration} is effectively equivalent to the \textbf{Markovnikov hydration} process (\textit{\autoref{core:mark_hydra}}), in which \ce{H2O} is added to remove an alkene double bond. However, oxymercuration--demercuration \textbf{avoids carbocation} formation, which prevents unintended rearranged alcohol products and yields a purer product mixture.

        The process employs mercury(II) acetate and sodium borohydride in a two-step sequence to achieve \ce{H2O} addition.

        \reactionfig{
          \scheme{
            \chemfig{
              *5(--
              (-[:0]\ce{CH3})
              =--)
            }
            \qquad
            \arrow{->[
              \shortstack{
                1. \textcolor{blue}{Hg}\ce{(OAc)2}, \textcolor{red}{HO}H \\
                2. \ce{NaBH4}
              }
            ]}
            \qquad
            \chemfig{
              *5(--
              (-[:15]\ce{CH3})
              (-[:-45]\textcolor{Red}{OH})
              ---)
            }
          }
        }{Oxymercuration--demercuration chemical reaction}{fig:ph35}{0.75}

        \begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]

          \item \textbf{Oxymercuration} 

          Similar to the \textbf{alkene halogenation} process (\textit{\autoref{core:halogenation}}), the large mercury atom forms a bridged, unevenly distributed mercurinium ion rather than a full carbocation, forcing the subsequent \ce{H2O} attack to occur strictly at the more substituted carbon.

          \item \textbf{Demercuration}

          Afterward, \ce{NaBH4} acts as a reducing agent that cleaves the C--Hg bond and replaces it with a hydrogen.

        \end{enumerate}

        Likewise, the use of \ce{ROH} allows for the addition of an \ce{-OR} group, just as \ce{H2O} adds a \ce{-OH} group.

          \scheme{

    \subsubsection{Hydroboration-Oxidations}

      \begin{core}[label=core:alkene_hydro_oxi]{Hydroboration-Oxidations}

        \textbf{Hydroboration-oxidation} is a two-step method by which chemists add a molecule of \ce{H2O} across the double bond of an alkene, with the hydroxyl group installed at the \textbf{less substituted carbon} (\textit{recall that Markovnikov hydration and oxymercuration--demercuration instead deliver \ce{H2O} to the more substituted carbon}). In this process, \ce{BH3} reacts regioselectively with three equivalents of alkene, and the resulting trialkylborane (\ce{BR3}) is subsequently oxidized and cleaved to yield three equivalents of \ce{ROH}.

        \reactionfig{
          \scheme{
            \chemfig{

            \qquad

              [:0]R
              -[:30]
              -[:-30]
              -[:30]OH
            }
          }
        }{Outline reaction of hydroboration-oxidation}{fig:ph38}{0.9}

        \begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]

          \item \textbf{Hydroboration} 

          When treated with \ce{BH3}, three equivalents of alkene bond to the borane such that the \textbf{less substituted carbon attaches to boron}, a regiochemical outcome that arises from two major factors.

          \reactionfig{
            \scheme{
              \chemfig{
                [:0]
                -[:30]
                (-[:90])
                -[:-30]
                -[:30]
                =[:-30]
              }
              \+
              \chemfig{
                \ce{BH3}
              }
              \arrow{->}
              \chemfig{
                [:0]
                -[:30]
                (-[:90])
                -[:-30]
                -[:30]
                -[:-30]
                -[:30]B
                (-[:90]R) 
                (-[:-30]R) 
              }
            }
          }{Hydroboration of terminal alkene; \textit{mind that the R groups in the product is the same is the one fully displayed alkyl substituent}; \textit{notice that the boron attaches to the less hindered end of the terminal alkene double bond}}{fig:ph39}{0.8}

          \begin{itemize}

            \item \textbf{Transition State Stabilization}

            As the \ce{e^-} rich π bond of the alkene attacks the empty, unhybridized p orbital of boron, the two alkene carbons do not share the resulting electronic burden equally. The carbon \textbf{not} forming the C--B bond progressively loses electron density, developing a partial positive charge ($\delta^+$) in the transition state. Consequently, when the less substituted carbon forms the C--B bond, the more substituted carbon is left to stabilize this developing charge through hyperconjugation, yielding a more stable transition state than the alternative regiochemical outcome.

            \reactionfig{
              \scheme{
                \chembrackets{
                  \chemfig{
                    [:0]C
                    (
                    -[@{one}:-90,,,,dotted]B
                    (-[:-90]\textcolor{White}{H})
                    (-[:-120]H)
                    -[@{sbond}:0]H
                    )
                    (<:[:135])
                    (<[:-150])
                    =[@{dbond}:0]\charge{100:4pt=$\scriptstyle δ+$}{C}
                    (-[@{two}:-90,,,,dotted]H)
                    (<:[:45])
                    (<[:-45])
                  }
                }
              }
              \chemmovearrow{
                \reactionfigarrow{dbond}{-80}{one}{-10}{Red}
                \reactionfigarrow{sbond}{100}{two}{170}{Red}
              }
            }{Hydroboration transition state}{fig:ph39two}{0.9}

            \item \textbf{Bond Polarization}

            A second, more subtle factor is the inherent electronegativity difference between boron ($\approx 2.0$) and hydrogen ($\approx 2.1$), which polarizes the B--H bond such that boron carries a slight positive charge ($\delta^+$) and hydrogen a slight negative charge ($\delta^-$). Consequently, the slightly electropositive boron preferentially pairs with the \ce{e^-} rich attack from the less substituted alkene carbon, while the slightly electronegative hydrogen preferentially pairs with the developing partial positive charge on the more substituted alkene carbon.

          \end{itemize}

          Another defining feature of hydroboration is the \textbf{syn} stereospecificity of the resulting alkylborane. Because the boron and hydrogen attacked by the alkene's π orbital are physically bonded to one another, they are delivered to the double bond simultaneously, constraining them to add to the same face of the alkene.

          \reactionfig{
            \scheme{
              \chemfig{
                *5(--
                (-[:0]\ce{CH3})
                =
                (-[:80]\ce{CH3})
                --)
              }
              \arrow{->[][]}
              \chemfig{
                *5(--

                (<[:60,,,,Red]\textcolor{Blue}{H})

                --)
              }
              \arrow{->[\textcolor{Red}{\ce{H2O2}}, \ce{^-OH}]}
              \chemfig{
                *5(--
                (<:[:45]\ce{CH3})
                (
                <[:-45,,,,Red]\textcolor{Red}{OH}
                )
                -
                (<[:60,,,,Red]\textcolor{Blue}{H})
                (<:[:100]\ce{H3C})
                --)
              }
            }
          }{Syn addition of B--H}{fig:ph40}{0.9}

          \item \textbf{Oxidation}

          The resulting alkylborane (\ce{R3B}) is then oxidized by a solution of \ce{H2O2} and \ce{^{-}OH}, yielding a trialkyl borate (\ce{(RO)3B}) via the alkyl-shift mechanism shown below.

          \reactionfig{
            \scheme{
              \chemfig{
                [:0]@{boron}\textcolor{Blue}{B}
                (-[:180]R)
                (-[:60]R)
                (-[:-60]R)
              }
              \+
              \chemname{
                \chemfig{
                  [:0]@{oxygen1}\textcolor{Red}{\ncharge{135}{O}}

                }

              \arrow{->}

              \reactionfigarrow{bond2}{-100}{oxygen3}{-80}{Red}
            }
          }{Oxidization of \ce{BR3}}{fig:ph41}{0.9}

          Finally, base-assisted hydrolysis of \ce{(RO)3B} by \ce{H2O} furnishes the three desired \ce{ROH} molecules, with a borate ion generated as a byproduct.

          \bce{\ce{(RO)3B + 3NaOH ->[H2O] Na3BO3 + 3ROH}}

        \end{enumerate}

        This transformation is often termed \textbf{anti-Markovnikov hydration}, since it adds a molecule of \ce{H2O} across the alkene but places the \ce{-OH} group on the less (\textit{rather than more}) substituted π-bond carbon.

      \end{core}

  \begin{core}{Triple Bond Strength}

    Combustion of ethyne (\ce{HC#CH}) releases a substantial $-311\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$ of heat. Compared to ethane (\ce{CH3CH3}), which releases $-373\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$, ethyne releases proportionally more energy per carbon, since none of that energy must first be spent breaking the four additional C--H bonds present in ethane.

    \bce{\ce{HC#CH + 2.5O2 -> 2CO2 + H2O}\hspace{5mm}$ΔH^{\circ} = -311\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$}

    \bce{\ce{CH3CH3 + 3.5O2 -> 2CO2 + 3H2O}\hspace{5mm}$ΔH^{\circ} = -373\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$}

    Because alkyne π bonds exist under a higher degree of strain, complete hydrogenation of both alkyne π bonds releases more energy than hydrogenation of two analogous alkene π bonds would, which is roughly $-60\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$. Notice that both terminal and internal hydrogenation have $ΔH^{\circ} < -60\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$.

    \bce{(\textit{terminal}) \ce{CH3CH2C#CH + 2H2 ->[catalyst][] CH3CH2CH2CH3}\hspace{5mm}$ΔH^{\circ} = -69.9\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$}

    \bce{(\textit{internal}) \ce{CH3C#CCH3 + 2H2 ->[catalyst][] CH3CH2CH2CH3}\hspace{5mm}$ΔH^{\circ} = -65.1\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$}

    Note the difference in net energy release: the internal triple bond is more stable than the terminal one owing to greater hyperconjugative stabilization, thus the internal scenario consumes more energy in order to break the same bonds, hence the lesser energy release compared to the terminal scenario.

  \end{core}

  \begin{core}[label=core:alkyne_acidity]{Relative Acidity of Alkynes}

    In typical hydrocarbons (\textit{alkanes and alkenes}), the C--H bond is considered essentially non-acidic. Terminal alkyne C--H bonds, however, defy this generalization: when treated with a sufficiently strong base, the terminal \ce{H+} can be removed.

      \begin{core}{Radical Hydrobromination}

        In alkynes, \textbf{radical hydrobromination} is the \textbf{single addition of HBr} via a peroxide-generated (\ce{ROOR}) radical mechanism. The reaction follows the same mechanism described for the alkene version of this electrophilic addition (\textit{\autoref{core:alkene_radical_hbr}}), but yields a mixture of products owing to free rotation about the newly formed double bond.

        \doublereactionfig{
          \scheme{
            \chemfig{
              [:0]
              -[:30]
              -[:-30]
              (-[:-90])
              -[:30]
              -[:-30]
              ~[:-30]
            }
            \arrow{->[HBr][ROOR]}
            \chembrackets{
              \chemfig{
                [:0]
                -[:30]
                -[:-30]
                (-[:-90])
                -[:30]
                -[:-30]\textcolor{Blue}{\charge{-120:4pt=$\cdot$}{}}
                =[:0]
                -[:30]Br
              }
            }
            \arrow{<->}
            \chembrackets{
              \chemfig{
                [:0]
                -[:0]
                -[:45]
                (-[:135])
                -[:0]
                -[:45]\textcolor{Blue}{\charge{120:4pt=$\cdot$}{}}
                =[:0]
                -[:45]Br
              }
            }
          }
        }{
          \scheme{
            \arrow{->[HBr][-Br$\cdot$]}
            \chemfig{
              [:0]
              -[:30]
              -[:-30]
              (-[:-90])
              -[:30]
              -[:-30]
              (-[:-90,,,,Blue]\textcolor{Blue}{H})
              =[:30]
              -[:90,,,,Blue]\textcolor{Blue}{Br}
            }
            \+
            \chemfig{
              [:0]
              -[:0]
              -[:45]
              (-[:135])
              -[:0]
              -[:45]
              (-[:135,,,,Blue]\textcolor{Blue}{H})
              =[:0]
              -[:45,,,,Blue]\textcolor{Blue}{Br}
            }
          }
        }{Radical hydrobromination}{fig:ph96}{0.7}

        Unlike the additions seen in \ce{HX} anti addition (\textit{\autoref{core:hx_anti_addition}}) and \ce{X2} anti addition (\textit{\autoref{core:xx_anti_addition}}), this reaction does not necessarily proceed through a second iteration to further convert the resulting alkene into an alkane. This is because the mechanism requires an electron-rich π bond, a condition the resulting bromoalkene fails to meet: the highly electronegative bromine substituent withdraws \ce{e^-} density from the double bond (\textit{a condition that does not affect an unsubstituted alkene}). Consequently, because the starting alkyne is considerably more reactive than the bromoalkene product, the radicals preferentially consume all remaining alkyne before reacting further with the bromoalkene intermediate.

        If, however, alkynes are exposed to an excess of \ce{HBr} and peroxides, a second radical addition can occur, governed by the same regiochemical stability considerations, to form a \textbf{geminal dihaloalkane}---the same product obtained from \ce{HX} anti addition (\textit{\autoref{core:hx_anti_addition}}). This makes the additional peroxide unnecessary if the original goal was simply to form a geminal dihaloalkane.

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}{Hydroboration-Oxidation}

        Alkyne \textbf{hydroboration-oxidation} employs a bulky borane such as \ce{R2BH} (\textit{where R is a bulky group, e.g., cyclohexyl}) to ensure that only one π bond undergoes hydroboration. \textit{Recall that alkene hydroboration-oxidation (\autoref{core:alkene_hydro_oxi}) simply uses \ce{BH3}, since there is only a single π bond to begin with.} The mechanism otherwise follows the same process as the alkene version of this reaction (\textit{\autoref{core:alkene_hydro_oxi}}) and effectively achieves an anti-Markovnikov hydration.

        \reactionfig{
          \scheme{
            \chemfig{
              [:0]RC
              ~[:0]CH

            \+
            \chemfig{
              [:0]\textcolor{Blue}{B}
              (-[:135]R)
              (-[:-135]R)
              -[:0,,,,Blue]\textcolor{Blue}{H}

            \arrow{->}
            \chemfig{
              [:0]C
              (-[:135]R)
              (-[:-135]\textcolor{Blue}{H})
              =[:0]C
              (-[:45]H)
              (-[:-45]\textcolor{Blue}{B}\ce{R2})
            }
            \qquad
            \arrow{->[\ce{H2O2}, \ce{^-OH}]}
            \qquad
            \chembrackets{
              \chemfig{
                [:0]C
                (-[:135]R)
                (-[:-135]H)

                (-[:-45]OH)
