\section{Alkenes}
  \label{sec:alkenes}

  \begin{core}{Alkene Orbital Structure}

    The orbital structure of an alkene double bond is a combination of a σ bond and a π bond. Compared to the alkane single bond length of roughly $1.5\,A$, the alkene double bond is shorter at approximately $1.3\,A$.

    \myfig{0.8}{alkene_alkyne_figures/alkene_sigma_pi}{caption}{fig:ph1}

  \end{core}

  \begin{core}{Alkene Bond Strength}

    The π bond of an alkane is relatively weaker than the its partner σ bond because its corresponding antibonding π$^*$ orbital sits closer to the bonding π orbital than the antibonding σ$^*$ orbital with the σ bonding orbital. As a result, breaking an alkene π bond takes roughly $65\,kcal\,mol^{-1}$ whereas the σ bond $108\,kcal\,mol^{-1}$.

    \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/alkene_mo_diagram}{caption}{fig:ph2}

    When compared with alkanes, the typical alkane σ C--C bond strength is roughly $88\,kcal\,mol^{-1}$. Notice that the alkene σ bond is stronger than the alkane σ bond because the alkene σ bond uses $sp^2$ hybridized orbitals compared to the $sp^3$ orbitals of alkanes, as shown in FIGURE REFERENCE. This higher s orbital characteristic---specifically 33\% vs 25\%, respectively---means greater bond strength \ce{e^-}s are pulled in closer relative to p orbitals.

    \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/alkene_bond_strength}{this may have to be made again}{fig:ph3}

    Thus in the context of the $pK_a$ values of substituent hydrogens of alkenes and alkanes, the alkenes show lower values compared to that of alkanes.

    <chemfig of ethene and ethane with pKa>

    Therefore, in principle, the following reaction between methyllithium and an alkene will reach a product favoring equilibrium because the alkene is more acidic (\textit{able to relaase a proton better}) than the alkane.

    \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/methyllithium}{caption}{fig:ph4}

    However, in practice, the reaction in FIGURE REFERENCE will face regio- and stereoselectivity issues because the other substitutent hydrogens can equally or more favorably dissociate compared to the one shown. In practice, chemists utilize the formation of \textbf{organometallics} (\textit{\autoref{alc-core:organometallics}}) to overcome these problems.

    \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/orgmet}{caption}{fig:ph5}

  \end{core}

  \subsection{Alkene Synthesis}

    \begin{core}{Elimination \& Saytzev Rule}

      Synthesizing alkenes from alkanes through E2 elimination requires the consideration of several factors.

      \begin{enumerate}

        \item \textbf{Base Size}

        Consider an E2 reaction \textbf{with a small base} on the following RX alkane.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/e2_small}{caption}{fig:ph10}

        Notice the favoring of the internal double bond alkene compared to the terminal version because the advantages of hyperoconjugation win over the minimal effects of steric hinderance due ot the use of a small base. In contrast, an E2 reaction \textbf{using large base} (\textit{BELOW FIGURE REFERENCE}) favors the terminal version because steric hinderance exerts a comparatively larger influence on transition state stability enough to override to hyperconjugation favorability of the internal double bond product.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/e2_large}{caption}{fig:ph11}

        This phenomenon is termed the \textbf{Saytzev Rule}, which states that non-bulky bases lead to more stable internal double bonds. The corresponding energy diagrams of the two reactions are shown below to visually depict the energy levels of the reaction pariticpants throughotu the reaction.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/e2_energy_dia}{caption}{fig:ph12}

        \item \textbf{Stereoselectivity}

        The presence of \textbf{multiple stereoisomers}---specifically among the formed internal double bond alkenes when utilizing E2 reactions with small bases---is something a chemist must also consider. Although the E2 reaction is stereoselective, favoring the more stable trans isomers over the sterically hindered cis isomers, the relationship is not absolute (\textit{some vis isomers do form}).

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/isomer}{}{fig:ph13}

        \item \textbf{Other Factors}

        Other factors such as preferable precursor confirmations, namely rotamers, play a role as well.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/rotamer}{}{fig:ph14}

      \end{enumerate}

      Keep in mind that elimination is also \textbf{stereospecific} as shown in the following figure.

      \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/stereospecific}{}{fig:ph15}

    \end{core}

  \subsection{Alkene Addition Reactions}

    \begin{core}{Alkene Addition Reactions}

      Alkenes are the fundamental building blocks for organic synthesis and the preparation of polymer materials. Their defining feature is the \textbf{unsaturated π bond}, which reactions through \textbf{addition reactions}, wherein the π bond is broken and a diatomic or compound molecule is added across the relevant carbon atoms of the former alkene.

      \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/alkene_addition_outline}{}{fig:ph20}

      Generally speaking, alkene addition reactions are $ΔH^{\circ} < 0$, however since the nature of the reaction is a combination reaction wherein two reactant molecules form a single product molecule, $ΔS^{\circ} < 0$, making it entropically unfavorable. In most reactions, the enthalpic favorability overrides the magnitude of entropic unfavorability. However, in some reactions or at high temperatures, the ΔG$^{\circ}$ turns positive, making these addition reactions thermodynamically unfavorable.

      \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/ethane_addition}{}{fig:ph21}

    \end{core}
    
    \subsubsection{Catalytic Hydrogenation}

      \begin{core}[label=core:hydrogenation]{Catalytic Hydrogenation}

        \textbf{Catalytic hydrogenation} refers to the addition of \ce{H2} to an alkene, effectively removing the alkene double bond and converting the target molecule into an alkane if the double bond in question was the only one. This process alone would require enormous amounts of pressure and extreme temperatures to occur, however \textbf{catalysts}---such as Pd/C or \ce{PtO2}---enable a mechanism with lower E$_{\text{a}}$ that chemists frequently use.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/cata_hydro}{}{fig:ph22}

        It's also important to note that the hydrogens are \textbf{stereospecifically} added to the \textbf{same side (syn)} of the target double bond. As a result, the outputed product mixutre will be \textbf{racemic}.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/cata_hydro2}{}{fig:ph23}

      \end{core}

    \subsubsection{Electrophilic Additions}

      \begin{core}{Eletrophilic Additions}

        Because the π bond is \textbf{\ce{e^-} rich}, it readily \textbf{reacts with a polar reagent A$^{\text{δ}^+}$--B$^{\text{δ}^-}$}, where the partially positive end A$^{\text{δ}^+}$ is drawn to the \ce{e^-} rich double bond.

        In the scenario where A$^{\text{δ}^+}$ is a \ce{H+}, the reaction that ensues is simply the reverse of an E1.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/elec_add}{indicate anti and syn stereoisomers in caption}{fig:ph24}

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}[label=core:hydrohalogenation]{Hydrohalogenation}
  
        \textbf{Hydrohalogenation} is a type of electrophilic addition wherein a \textbf{HX is added} to the alkene double bond where X is a halogen.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/hydrohalo}{}{fig:ph25}

        Notice that the \ce{H+} adds to the \textbf{less substituted} carbon because it results in a more substituted carbocation in the other carbon, favoring $\text{tertiary} > \text{secondary} > \text{primary}$ carbocation formation. This phenomenon is called \textbf{Markovnikov's Rule} and the following energy diagram for the different stereospecific pathways depicts the stability of the different transition states nicely.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/hydrohalo2}{}{fig:ph26}

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}[label=core:mark_hydra]{Markovnikov Hydration}

        \textbf{Markovnikov hydration} is refers to the conversion of an alkene to an alcohol through the \textbf{addition of \ce{H2O}} in the presence of an acid catalyst---the reverse of the alkene synthesis reaction covered in \autoref{core:alkene_roh}.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/mark_hydra}{}{fig:ph27}

        The underlying mechanism to the reaction is shown in the following. Notice how the \ce{H+} is regenerated by the end of the reaction.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/mark_hydra2}{}{fig:ph28}

        There are, however, bound to be a mixture of alkenes, transient intermediaries, and alcohols because Markovnikov hydration is at dynamic equilibrium with the pathway previously covered in \autoref{core:alkene_roh}. Because the temporary presence of a carbocation introduces hydride shifts, catalytic \ce{H+} will turn a single alkene species into a slurry of stereoisomeric alkenes, with ratios determined by relative thermodynamic stabilties primarily based on steric hinderance and hyperconjugation.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/mark_hydra3}{}{fig:ph29}

        The full mechanism of equilibrium between an alcohol and its corresponding alkene products is shown in the follwing chemical equation using butanol/butene as an example.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/mark_hydra4}{caption includes: leads to the rapid equilibrium of three alkene products}{fig:ph30}

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}[label=core:halogenation]{Halogenation}

        \textbf{Halogenation} is the addition of a diatomic halogen molecule \ce{X2} to an alkene double bond. In contrast to the addition of HX (\textit{\autoref{core:hydrohalogenation}})---which is inherently polarized due to the electronegativity differences---\ce{X2} is not polarized inherently, but rather \textbf{gets polarized during its apporach to the \ce{e^-} rich alkene double bond}.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/halo}{}{fig:ph31}

        The underlying mechanism is shown in the following figure.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/halo2}{}{fig:ph32}

        Notice the formation of a halonium ring because the alternative carbocation state will want to distribute the burden of its positive charge. Because the second attack to the bridged halonium exclusively occurs to the backside of the first halogen attack, halogenation is \textbf{stereospecifically anti (not syn)}. Likewise, if the second attack has thermodynamically equal possibility of attack either backside carbon---meaning that the inital alkene was perfectly \textbf{symmetrical}---the ensuing products will be an \textbf{racemic mixture}.

        If the initial alkene is \textbf{asymmetrical}, the halonium ion intermediate is actually highly distorted, wherein the bond between the halogen and more substituted carbon is \textbf{longer}. Likewise, as the halogen pulls more \ce{e^-} density towards itself the more substituted carbon is able possess a greater δ$^+$ due to hyperconjugation. As a result, thehalonium ring transition state \textbf{already has a high degree of carbocation} behavior and thus mimics more of a \sno attack characteristic although the backside attack is \snt by definition because of its exclusively anti result. Consequently, the backside nucleophilic attack occurs at the more substituted carbon, an interaction wherein the impact of charge is greater than steric hinderance on regioselectivity.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/halo3}{}{fig:ph33}

        On a different note, the intermediary halonium ring ions can also be intercepted by other nucleophiles such as \ce{HOH} (\textit{FIG REFERENCE ABOVE}) or \ce{ROH} (\textit{FIG REFERENCE BELOW}).

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/halo4}{}{fig:ph34}

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}[label=core:alkene_oxymercuration]{Oxymercuration--Demercuration}

        \textbf{Oxymercuration--demercuration} is effectively the same as the \textbf{Markovnikov hydration} (\textit{\autoref{core:mark_hydra}}) process wherein a \ce{H2O} is added to remove an alkene double bond. However, oxymercuration--demercuration \textbf{avoids carbocation} formation, which prevents the unintended rearranged product alcohols and produces a purer product mix.

        The process utlizes mercury (II) acetate and sodium borohydride in a two step chemical process to achieve its \ce{H2O} addition.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/oxdemerc}{}{fig:ph35}

        <figure of simple H2O equation>

        \begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]

          \item \textbf{Oxymercuration} 

          Similar to the \textbf{alkene halogenation} (\textit{\autoref{core:halogenation}}) process, the large mercury atom forms a bridged, unevenly distributed mercurinium ion rather than a full carbocation, forcing the subsequent \ce{H2O} attack to strictly occur at the more substituted carbon.

          \item \textbf{Demercuration}

          Afterwards, the \ce{NaBH4} acts as a reducing agent that cleaves the C--Hg bondand replaces it with a simple hydrogen atom.

        \end{enumerate}

        Likewise, the usage of \ce{ROH} allows the addition of a \ce{-OR} group just as how a \ce{-OH} was added by \ce{H2O}.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/oxdemerc2}{}{fig:ph36}

        When compared to Markovnikov hydration (\textit{\autoref{core:mark_hydra}}), oxymercuration--demercuration is the preferred method used by chemists in order to convert an alkene to an alcohol due to the purity of the resulting product mix.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/oxdemerc3}{}{fig:ph37}

      \end{core}

    \subsubsection{Hydroboration-Oxidations}

      \begin{core}[label=core:alkene_hydro_oxi]{Hydroboration-Oxidations}

        \textbf{Hydroboration-oxidation} is a method chemists use to \textbf{add \ce{H2O}} to a double bond of an alkene at the \textbf{less substituted carbon of the double bond} (\textit{remember that the Markovnikov hydration and oxymercuration--demercuration \ce{H2O} additions occur at the more substituted carbon}). This process utilizes \ce{BH3} to regioselectively bond to 3 alkenes and subsequently oxidate and cleave the resulting \ce{BR3} to produce 3 \ce{ROH}.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/hydrobor}{}{fig:ph38}

        \begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]

          \item \textbf{Hydroboration} 

          When mixed with \ce{BH3}, three alkenes bond to \ce{BH3} with the \textbf{less substituted carbon paired to the boron}, occuring due to two major factors.

          \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/hydrobor2}{}{fig:ph39}

          \begin{itemize}

            \item \textbf{Transition State Stabilization}

            When the \ce{e^-} rich π bond of the alkene reaches out to attack the empty unhybridized p oribital of the boron, the two carbon atoms of the double bond do not share teh burden equally. The carbon that is \textbf{not} forming the C--B bond begins to lose its \ce{e^-} density, creating a partial positive charge δ$^+$ during its transition state. Consequently, if the less substituted carbon form the C--B bond, the more substituted carbon can better stabilize the this charge through hyperconjugation, allowing for a more stable transition state relative to the alternative C--B bond formation.

            <need to make figure>

            \item \textbf{Bond Polarization}

            Another subtle factor that plays a role is the inherent electronegativity difference between boron (~2.0) and hydrogen (~2.1), creating a polarized bond where boron is slightly positive (δ$^+$) and hydrogen is slightly negative (δ$^-$). As a result, the slightly positive boron more favorably pairs with the \ce{e^-} rich attack from the less substituted alkene carbon and the slightly negative hydrogen more favorably pairs with the subsequently partial positive non-boron bonding alkene carbon.

            <need to make figure>

          \end{itemize}

          Another important characteristic of hydrobromination is the \textbf{syn} stereospecificity of the resulting alkylborane. The B and H that are attacked by an alkene π orbital are physcially attached to one another and added to the double bond simultaneously, forcing them to attach to the same face of the alkene.

          \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/hydrobor3}{}{fig:ph40}

          \item \textbf{Oxidation}

          The resulting alkylborane \ce{R3B} is then oxidized by a solution of \ce{H2O2} and \ce{^{-}OH} which yields trialkyl borate (\ce{(RO)3B}) through the following alkyl shift mechanism.

          \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/hydrobor4}{}{fig:ph41}

          Finally, \ce{H2O} hydrolyzes the \ce{(RO)3B} with the help of a base to form the intended 3 ROH molecules with a borate ion as a byproduct.

          \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/hydrobor5}{}{fig:ph42}

        \end{enumerate}

        Often this entire process is called \textbf{anti-Markovnikov hydration} because it adds a \ce{H2O} molecule to the alkene but does so in a manner where the \ce{-OH} group ends up on the less substituted π bond carbon rather than the more substituted one.

      \end{core}

    \subsubsection{Electrophilic Carbene Additions}

      \begin{core}[label=core:carbene_synthesis]{Carbene Synthesis}

        \textbf{Carbenes} (:\ce{CR2}) are sp$^2$ hybridized \textbf{6 \ce{e^-}} species with a lone pair and empty p orbital---the parent of which is methylene (:\ce{CH2}). They can be thought of as being derived by \textbf{deprotonating carbocations}.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/carbene}{}{fig:ph43}

        Because they are \ce{e^-} deficient, carbenes are extermely unstable, short lived intermediates that act as powerful \textbf{electrophiles}. The following are the three primary pathways chemists use to generate these intermediates in the laboratory.

        \begin{enumerate}

          \item \textbf{Methylene from diazomethane (\ce{CH2N2})}

          \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/carbene2}{}{fig:ph44}

          Mind that this reaction is practically irreversible because the resulting \ce{N2} is incredibly stable, driving the reaction forward to cleanly leave behind highly reactive methylene.

          \item \textbf{Dichlorocarbene from chloroform}

          \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/carbene3}{}{fig:ph45}

          The above reaction utilizes α-elimination (\textit{distinct from the standard β-eliminations E1 or E2 normally used to make alkenes}) through the strong base \ce{(CH3)3CO-} and the departure of a highly electronegative chlorine atom.

          \item \textbf{Simmsons-Smith reagent}

          As mentioned previously, free methylene is so wildly reactive that it can be incredibly dangerous to work with. The Simmsons-Smith reaction provides a much safer, controlled workaround.

          \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/carbene4}{}{fig:ph46}

          The treatment of diiodomethane with a zinc-copper couple forms an organometallic species that behaves like a hybrid between a stable molecule and a methylene like state.

        \end{enumerate}

        \textbf{Radical hydrobromination} refers to the \textbf{addition of an HBr} molecule to an alkene double bond with the assistance of \ce{RO-OR} in an \textbf{anti-Markovnikov manner}, meaning that the Br is attached to the \textbf{less substituted carbon}. Recall that regular \textbf{hydrohalogenation} (\textit{\autoref{core:hydrohalogenation}}) follows Markovnikov regiochemistry where the halogen is attached to the more substituted carbon.

        The underlying mechanism is shown in the following figure, started by peroxide radical initiators (\textit{the O--O bond is weak at around $39\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$})

        \begin{enumerate}

          \item \textbf{Initiation Steps} 

          \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/radbro2}{}{fig:ph66}

          \item \textbf{Propagation Steps}

          \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/radbro3}{seperate propagation I and II in the first}{fig:ph67}

          Notice that the Br$\cdot$ is added to the less substituted carbon, resulting in the resulting carbon radical to be better hyperconjugated than the alternative binding arrangement due to being more substituted.

        This radical hydrobromination does not work for HCl or HI because one of the propagation steps are \textbf{endothermic} due the unique electronegative properties of Cl and I. Br, on the other hand, sits at the thermodynamic sweet spot between Cl and I. RSH anti-markovnikov addition works in the same manner as Br, sitting in that same sweet spot.

\section{Alkynes}
  \label{sec:alkynes}

  \begin{core}{Alkyne Orbital Structure}

    An alkyne triple bond is composed of two perpendicular π bonds and a conjoined sp hybrid σ bond.

    \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/alkyne}{}{fig:ph68}

  \end{core}

  \begin{core}{Triple Bond Strength}

    Ethyne releases a massive $-311\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$ of heat when combusted. Compared to ethane (\ce{CH3CH3})---which releases $-373\text{ kcal/mol}$---ethyne releases a proportionally greater amount of energy on a per carbon basis because you don't have to spend extra energy to break the 4 additional C--H bonds in ethane.

    \bce{\ce{HC#CH + 2.5O2 -> 2CO2 + H2O}\hspace{5mm}$ΔH^{\circ} = -311\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$}

    \bce{\ce{CH3CH3 + 3.5O2 -> 2CO2 + 3H2O}\hspace{5mm}$ΔH^{\circ} = -373\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$}

    Due to the higher level of strain alkyne π bonds exist under, the full hydrogenation of the two alkyne π bonds releases more energy than that of two alkene π bonds (\textit{which would be roughly $-60\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$})

    \bce{(\textit{terminal}) \ce{CH3CH2C#CH + 2H2 ->[catalyst][] CH3CH2CH2CH3}\hspace{5mm}$ΔH^{\circ} = -69.9\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$}

    \bce{(\textit{internal}) \ce{CH3C#CCH3 + 2H2 ->[catalyst][] CH3CH2CH2CH3}\hspace{5mm}$ΔH^{\circ} = -65.1\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$}

    Notice the differences in net energy release due to the greater stability of the internal triple bond over the terminal version because of hyperconjugation.

  \end{core}

  \begin{core}[label=core:alkyne_acidity]{Relative Acidity of Alkynes}

    In general hydrocarbons (alkanes and alkenes), the $\text{C–H}$ bond is considered completely non-acidic. However, terminal alkyne C--H bonds defy this rule when treated with a sufficiently strong base, wherein the terminal \ce{H+} can be plucked off.

    \bce{\ce{RC#C-H + B- -> R-C#C- + HB}\hspace{5mm}$pK_a = 25$}

    \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/alkyne2}{}{fig:ph69}

    The above acidic trend is explained by the greater s orbital characteristics of the corresponding orbital hybridization. Since an s-orbital is spherical and sits much closer to the positively charged carbon nucleus than a directional, elongated $p$-orbital,the lone pair of electrons left behind after deprotonation is held very close to the positive nucleus. This electrostatic proximity stabilizes the negative charge immensely.making the corresponding alkyne conjugate base comparatively more stable.

    Using this alkyne acidity, chemists are able to execute the following example applications

      \item \textbf{Organolithium Reagents} 

      \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/alkyne3}{}{fig:ph70}

      \item \textbf{Sodium Amide (\ce{NaNH2})}

      \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/alkyne4}{}{fig:ph71}

    \end{enumerate}

  \end{core}

      \begin{core}{Radical Hydrobromination}

        \textbf{Radical hydrobromination} in alkynes is the \textbf{single addition of \ce{HBr}} through the formation of radical species with the assistance of \ce{ROOR}. The reaction follows the same mechanism covered in the alkene version of this electrophilic addition, covered in \autoref{core:alkene_radical_hbr}, however leads to the formation of product mixes due to the possible rotation along the resultingly formed double bond.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/alkyne_radhal}{}{fig:ph96}

        Unlike the additions we've seen in \ce{HX} anti addition (\textit{\autoref{core:hx_anti_addition}}) and \ce{X2} anti addition (\textit{\autoref{core:xx_anti_addition}}), this reaction does not nessecarily undergo a second iteration to convert the resulting alkene further into an alkane form. This is because the mechanism requires the presence of an \ce{e^-} dense π bond, which the resulting bromine-attached alkene does not satisfy because bromine is highly electronegative, pulling \ce{e^-} density away from the double bond (\textit{a clean alkene does not experience this}). As a result, because the starting alkyne is significantly more reactive than the bromoalkene product, the radicals will preferentially consume all the remaining alkyne before even attempting to touch the bromoalkene intermediate.

        If one exposes alkynes to an excess of \ce{HBr} and peroxides, however, a second radical addtion can occur, which follows the same Markovnikov stability rules to form a \textbf{geminal dihaloalkane}, the same product of \ce{HX} anti addition (\textit{\autoref{core:hx_anti_addition}}), making the extra peroxide use unnessecary if the orignal intention was to form a geminal dihaloalkane.

      \end{core}
