\section{Alkenes}
  \label{sec:alkenes}

  \begin{core}{Alkene Orbital Structure}

    The orbital structure of an alkene double bond arises from the combination of a σ bond and a π bond. This additional π overlap results in a shorter bond length relative to an alkane single bond: whereas a typical C--C single bond measures roughly $1.5\,A$, the C=C double bond is contracted to approximately $1.3\,A$.

    \myfig{1}{alkene_alkyne_figures/alkene_sigma_pi}{Alkene σ (\textit{left}) and π (\textit{right}) bonding orbitals}{fig:ph1}

  \end{core}

  \begin{core}{Alkene Bond Strength}

    The π bond of an alkene is relatively weaker than its partner σ bond because the antibonding π$^*$ orbital sits closer in energy to the bonding π orbital than the antibonding σ$^*$ orbital sits to the bonding σ orbital. As a result, breaking an alkene π bond requires roughly $65\,kcal\,mol^{-1}$, whereas breaking the σ bond requires $108\,kcal\,mol^{-1}$.

    \mofig{
      \AO[seven](3cm){s}{2.25;}
      \AO[four](3cm){s}{1.5;}
      \AO[five](3cm){s}{0.75}
      \AO[eight](3cm){s}{0}
      \node[right] at (seven.east) {σ$^*$};
      \node[right] at (four.east)  {π$^*$};
      \node[right] at (five.east)  {π};
      \node[right] at (eight.east) {σ};
      \EnergyAxis[title=E]
    }{Molecular bonding orbitals levels of σ, π, π$^*$, and σ$^*$ in an alkene double bond; \textit{notice that the distance between π and π$^*$ is less than that of σ and σ$^*$}}{fig:ph2}{1}

    By comparison, the typical alkane $\sigma$ C--C bond strength is roughly $88\,kcal\,mol^{-1}$. The alkene σ bond is therefore stronger than the alkane σ bond, since the alkene σ bond is formed from $sp^2$ hybridized orbitals rather than the $sp^3$ orbitals used in alkanes. This \textbf{greater s-orbital character}---33\% versus 25\%, respectively---corresponds to greater bond strength, as the bonding \ce{e^-}s are held closer to the nucleus relative to p orbitals.

    Consequently, when comparing the $pK_a$ values of the substituent hydrogens on alkenes and alkanes, alkenes exhibit lower $pK_a$ values than alkanes.

      }
    }{Butane isomer conversions to butane}{fig:ph6}{0.8}

    The greater relative stability of internal double bonds over terminal ones, and of trans over cis substituents, arises from two factors.

    \begin{enumerate}

      \item \textbf{Hyperconjugation} allows the temporary charge imbalance arising in the transition state to be better distributed among neighboring orbitals in the internal bond butene than in its terminal counterpart.

      \item \textbf{Steric hindrance} between the cis methyl groups increases the relative instability of the cis butene compared to its trans counterpart.

      \reactionfig{
        \scheme{
          \chemname{
            \chemfig{
              [:0]C
              (<:[:135]H)
              (<[:-135]\textcolor{Red}{\textbf{\ce{H3C}}})
              =[:0]C
              (<:[:45]H)
              (<[:-45]\textcolor{Red}{\textbf{\ce{CH3}}})
            }
          }{cis}
          \qquad \qquad \qquad
          \chemname{
            \chemfig{
              [:0]C
              (<:[:135]H)
              (<[:-135]\textcolor{Red}{\textbf{\ce{H3C}}})
              =[:0]C
              (<:[:45]\textcolor{Red}{\textbf{\ce{CH3}}})
              (<[:-45]H)
            }
          }{trans}
        }
      }{Steric hinderance between methyl groups of cis (\textit{left}) vs trans (\textit{right}) butene; \textit{more steric exists between methyl groups of cis butene because of closer methyl group proximity}}{fig:ph8}{0.8}

    \end{enumerate}

    The general order of alkene stability thus follows as shown below.
    \[
      \ce{CH2=CH2} \quad < \quad \ce{RCH=CH2} \quad < \quad \ce{RCH=CHR}_{\text{cis}} \quad < \quad \ce{RCH=CHR}_{\text{trans}} \quad < \quad \ce{RCH=CR2} \quad < \quad \ce{R2C=CR2}
    \]

  \end{core}

    \subsubsection{Hydroboration-Oxidations}

      \begin{core}[label=core:alkene_hydro_oxi]{Hydroboration-Oxidations}

        \textbf{Hydroboration-oxidation} is a method chemists use to \textbf{add \ce{H2O}} to a double bond of an alkene at the \textbf{less substituted carbon of the double bond} (\textit{remember that the Markovnikov hydration and oxymercuration--demercuration \ce{H2O} additions occur at the more substituted carbon}). This process utilizes \ce{BH3} to regioselectively bond to 3 alkenes and subsequently oxidate and cleave the resulting \ce{BR3} to produce 3 \ce{ROH}.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/hydrobor}{}{fig:ph38}

        \begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]

          \item \textbf{Hydroboration} 

          When mixed with \ce{BH3}, three alkenes bond to \ce{BH3} with the \textbf{less substituted carbon paired to the boron}, occuring due to two major factors.

          \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/hydrobor2}{}{fig:ph39}

          \begin{itemize}

            \item \textbf{Transition State Stabilization}

            When the \ce{e^-} rich π bond of the alkene reaches out to attack the empty unhybridized p oribital of the boron, the two carbon atoms of the double bond do not share teh burden equally. The carbon that is \textbf{not} forming the C--B bond begins to lose its \ce{e^-} density, creating a partial positive charge δ$^+$ during its transition state. Consequently, if the less substituted carbon form the C--B bond, the more substituted carbon can better stabilize the this charge through hyperconjugation, allowing for a more stable transition state relative to the alternative C--B bond formation.

            <need to make figure>

            \item \textbf{Bond Polarization}

            Another subtle factor that plays a role is the inherent electronegativity difference between boron (~2.0) and hydrogen (~2.1), creating a polarized bond where boron is slightly positive (δ$^+$) and hydrogen is slightly negative (δ$^-$). As a result, the slightly positive boron more favorably pairs with the \ce{e^-} rich attack from the less substituted alkene carbon and the slightly negative hydrogen more favorably pairs with the subsequently partial positive non-boron bonding alkene carbon.

            <need to make figure>

          \end{itemize}

          Another important characteristic of hydrobromination is the \textbf{syn} stereospecificity of the resulting alkylborane. The B and H that are attacked by an alkene π orbital are physcially attached to one another and added to the double bond simultaneously, forcing them to attach to the same face of the alkene.

          \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/hydrobor3}{}{fig:ph40}

          \item \textbf{Oxidation}

          The resulting alkylborane \ce{R3B} is then oxidized by a solution of \ce{H2O2} and \ce{^{-}OH} which yields trialkyl borate (\ce{(RO)3B}) through the following alkyl shift mechanism.

          \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/hydrobor4}{}{fig:ph41}

          Finally, \ce{H2O} hydrolyzes the \ce{(RO)3B} with the help of a base to form the intended 3 ROH molecules with a borate ion as a byproduct.

          \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/hydrobor5}{}{fig:ph42}

        \end{enumerate}

        Often this entire process is called \textbf{anti-Markovnikov hydration} because it adds a \ce{H2O} molecule to the alkene but does so in a manner where the \ce{-OH} group ends up on the less substituted π bond carbon rather than the more substituted one.

\section{Alkynes}
  \label{sec:alkynes}

  \begin{core}{Alkyne Orbital Structure}

    An alkyne triple bond is composed of two perpendicular π bonds and a conjoined sp hybrid σ bond.

    \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/alkyne}{}{fig:ph68}

  \end{core}

  \begin{core}{Triple Bond Strength}

    Ethyne releases a massive $-311\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$ of heat when combusted. Compared to ethane (\ce{CH3CH3})---which releases $-373\text{ kcal/mol}$---ethyne releases a proportionally greater amount of energy on a per carbon basis because you don't have to spend extra energy to break the 4 additional C--H bonds in ethane.

    \bce{\ce{HC#CH + 2.5O2 -> 2CO2 + H2O}\hspace{5mm}$ΔH^{\circ} = -311\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$}

    \bce{\ce{CH3CH3 + 3.5O2 -> 2CO2 + 3H2O}\hspace{5mm}$ΔH^{\circ} = -373\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$}

    Due to the higher level of strain alkyne π bonds exist under, the full hydrogenation of the two alkyne π bonds releases more energy than that of two alkene π bonds (\textit{which would be roughly $-60\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$})

    \bce{(\textit{terminal}) \ce{CH3CH2C#CH + 2H2 ->[catalyst][] CH3CH2CH2CH3}\hspace{5mm}$ΔH^{\circ} = -69.9\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$}

    \bce{(\textit{internal}) \ce{CH3C#CCH3 + 2H2 ->[catalyst][] CH3CH2CH2CH3}\hspace{5mm}$ΔH^{\circ} = -65.1\,\text{kcal}\,\text{mol}^{-1}$}

    Notice the differences in net energy release due to the greater stability of the internal triple bond over the terminal version because of hyperconjugation.

  \end{core}

  \begin{core}[label=core:alkyne_acidity]{Relative Acidity of Alkynes}

    In general hydrocarbons (alkanes and alkenes), the $\text{C–H}$ bond is considered completely non-acidic. However, terminal alkyne C--H bonds defy this rule when treated with a sufficiently strong base, wherein the terminal \ce{H+} can be plucked off.

    \bce{\ce{RC#C-H + B- -> R-C#C- + HB}\hspace{5mm}$pK_a = 25$}

    \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/alkyne2}{}{fig:ph69}

    The above acidic trend is explained by the greater s orbital characteristics of the corresponding orbital hybridization. Since an s-orbital is spherical and sits much closer to the positively charged carbon nucleus than a directional, elongated $p$-orbital,the lone pair of electrons left behind after deprotonation is held very close to the positive nucleus. This electrostatic proximity stabilizes the negative charge immensely.making the corresponding alkyne conjugate base comparatively more stable.

    Using this alkyne acidity, chemists are able to execute the following example applications

    \begin{enumerate}

      \item \textbf{Organolithium Reagents} 

      \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/alkyne3}{}{fig:ph70}

      \item \textbf{Sodium Amide (\ce{NaNH2})}

      \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/alkyne4}{}{fig:ph71}

    \end{enumerate}

  \end{core}

      \begin{core}{Radical Hydrobromination}

        \textbf{Radical hydrobromination} in alkynes is the \textbf{single addition of \ce{HBr}} through the formation of radical species with the assistance of \ce{ROOR}. The reaction follows the same mechanism covered in the alkene version of this electrophilic addition, covered in \autoref{core:alkene_radical_hbr}, however leads to the formation of product mixes due to the possible rotation along the resultingly formed double bond.

        \myfig{0.5}{alkene_alkyne_figures/alkyne_radhal}{}{fig:ph96}

        Unlike the additions we've seen in \ce{HX} anti addition (\textit{\autoref{core:hx_anti_addition}}) and \ce{X2} anti addition (\textit{\autoref{core:xx_anti_addition}}), this reaction does not nessecarily undergo a second iteration to convert the resulting alkene further into an alkane form. This is because the mechanism requires the presence of an \ce{e^-} dense π bond, which the resulting bromine-attached alkene does not satisfy because bromine is highly electronegative, pulling \ce{e^-} density away from the double bond (\textit{a clean alkene does not experience this}). As a result, because the starting alkyne is significantly more reactive than the bromoalkene product, the radicals will preferentially consume all the remaining alkyne before even attempting to touch the bromoalkene intermediate.

        If one exposes alkynes to an excess of \ce{HBr} and peroxides, however, a second radical addtion can occur, which follows the same Markovnikov stability rules to form a \textbf{geminal dihaloalkane}, the same product of \ce{HX} anti addition (\textit{\autoref{core:hx_anti_addition}}), making the extra peroxide use unnessecary if the orignal intention was to form a geminal dihaloalkane.

      \end{core}
