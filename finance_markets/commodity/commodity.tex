\section{Market Foundation}

  \begin{core}{Commodity Markets}

    Commodity markets deal primarily in \textbf{physical, tangible assets} that serve as \textbf{elementary inputs} for global economic production. Because these raw materials form the base of the manufacturing supply chain, significant price volatility or supply disruptions in these goods inevitably cascade down to consumer-end products. \textit{For instance, while wheat represents the underlying commodity traded in financial markets, commercial bread serves as the final consumer good.}

    Modern tradable commodities are systematically classified into three primary sectors.

    \begin{itemize}

      \item \textbf{Energy} (\textit{\autoref{sec:energy}}): Primary fuel resources, including crude oil, natural gas, and coal.

      \item \textbf{Metals} (\textit{\autoref{sec:metal}}): Industrial and mineral ores, spanning base, ferrous, and precious varieties.

      \item \textbf{Agriculture} (\textit{\autoref{sec:agriculture}}): Cultivated assets, comprising grains, oilseeds, soft commodities, and livestock.

    \end{itemize}

  \end{core}
  
  \begin{core}{Historical Development of Commodity Trading}

    Commodity trading represents one of the oldest forms of economic commerce, predating the development of \href{https://ibahng.github.io/finance_markets/equity/equity.pdf}{equity} and \href{https://ibahng.github.io/finance_markets/fixed_income/fixed_income.pdf}{fixed-income} markets by several millennia, with historical origins extending back to ancient Mesopotamia.

    Early transactions functioned as forward negotiations between parties to exchange a specific quantity of a commodity at a designated future date---a mechanism known today as an \textbf{over-the-counter (OTC)} contract. However, persistent disagreements regarding quality standards and delivery terms eventually drove the market toward standardization, giving birth to centralized futures trading. Early global milestones in this institutional evolution include the \textbf{Dojima Rice Exchange in Japan} (\textit{1697}), the \textbf{Chicago Board of Trade (CBOT)} (\textit{1848}) in the United States, and subsequently the \textbf{London Metal Exchange (LME)} (\textit{1877}) in Europe.

    Historically, these centralized marketplaces relied on a physical \textbf{open-outcry} system (\textit{\autoref{fig:pit}}), a framework where floor brokers executed transactions face-to-face within trading pits through synchronized shouting and hand signals.

    \myfig{0.75}{commodity_figures/pit}{Chicago Board of Trade open-outcry system termed "\textit{the Pit}"}{fig:pit}

    In the modern era, however, commodity execution has almost entirely transitioned to electronic platforms. As a result of this widespread digital shift, the LME remains unique as one of the final venues to preserve its legacy open-outcry system termed "\textit{the Ring}".

  \end{core}

  \begin{core}{Basic Supply \& Demand}

    Commodity markets are governed by the basic economic principles of \textbf{supply and demand} (\textit{\autoref{mic-sec:sup_dem}}). Within this framework, \textbf{physical storage} (\textit{\autoref{fig:japan_or}}) serves a critical stabilizing function, dampening price volatility and restoring market equilibrium during imbalances, such as an unexpected supply shock.

    \myfig{0.75}{commodity_figures/japan_or}{Japanese oil reserves; \textit{in March 2026, Japan announced the release of about 80 million barrels in response to the Middle East conflict in Iran, which has resulted in the disruption of oil flows through the \textbf{Strait of Hormuz}}}{fig:japan_or}

    Because production centers are rarely geographically aligned with primary consumption hubs, logistics play a defining role in market dynamics. Over 90\% of global commodity trade volume is transported via \textbf{maritime freight} (\textit{\autoref{fig:oil_ship}}), with the remainder distributed across pipelines, rail, trucking, and aviation---meaning that transportation costs represent a significant economic variable that heavily influences both trade routes and ultimate market destinations.

    \myfig{0.8}{commodity_figures/oil_ship}{Oil tanker}{fig:oil_ship}

    Accurate analysis of these dynamics, however, is complicated by significant \textbf{data acquisition challenges}: sovereign nations report production, consumption, and inventory figures with varying degrees of frequency, scope, and statistical reliability, requiring market analysts to dedicate substantial effort to modeling and estimating these baseline values.

  \end{core}

\section{Commodity Trading Mechanics}

  \subsection{Market Structure \& Price}

    \begin{core}[label=core:phy_pri]{Physical Pricing and OTC Structure}

      Commodity valuation requires the \textbf{simultaneous evaluation of multiple physical variables}, where variations in quality, grade, and geographic location heavily influence spot pricing.

      \myfig{0.55}{commodity_figures/crude_grade}{Basic crude grade spectrum}{fig:crude_grade}

      In the oil sector, for example, \textbf{hundreds of distinct crude grades} are traded globally, meaning the limited selection of standardized futures contracts \textbf{cannot always accommodate the precise needs of physical market participants}. Consequently, physical commodity commerce remains predominantly structured as a \textbf{bilateral business}, directly connecting buyers and sellers via customized OTC transactions. \textit{This contrasts with exchange-based markets, where standardized contracts enable multilateral participation among a large number of buyers and sellers but offer substantially less flexibility in contract design.}

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Clearing Houses and Counterparty Risk}

      Within \href{https://ibahng.github.io/finance_markets/derivatives/derivatives.pdf}{derivative} and commodity markets, a \textbf{clearing house} functions as a specialized \textbf{central counterparty (CCP)} explicitly designed to eliminate systemic counterparty credit risk. Upon the execution of a trade, the clearing house employs a legal mechanism known as \textbf{novation}, interposing itself between the original transacting parties. Through this process, the clearing house becomes the designated buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer, structurally \textbf{guaranteeing the fulfillment of the contract even in the event of a participant's default}.

      \myfig{0.5}{commodity_figures/cme}{The CME Group; \textit{the primary derivatives marketplace in the United States, also functions as a central counterparty clearinghouse for futures and options contracts traded across its exchanges}}{fig:cme}

      To prudently manage this massive assumption of systemic risk, clearing houses enforce a rigorous \textbf{collateral framework}. Market participants are required to post an initial margin as a baseline security deposit, and active positions are marked-to-market daily, necessitating variation margin adjustments to continuously settle real-time price fluctuations. This system is critical in highly leveraged and volatile asset classes, as it ensures liquidity and protects the broader financial system from cascading failures.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Reference Pricing and Benchmarking}

      To establish reliable baseline values amid the complexities outlined in \autoref{core:phy_pri}, the market relies on specialized \textbf{Price Reporting Agencies (PRAs)} such as \href{https://www.spglobal.com/energy/en}{\textbf{S\&P Global Platts}} or \href{https://www.argusmedia.com/en}{\textbf{Argus Media}}, which publish independent price assessments across a broad spectrum of contracts. These assessments provide critical transparency into the market, allowing the majority of physical OTC transactions to be executed at an agreed price differential \textbf{relative to these recognized benchmarks}.

      Furthermore, because \textbf{standardized financial futures do not exist for every unique physical grade}, market participants rely on these benchmarks as pricing proxies to manage asset exposure. In practice, a physical OTC agreement for a niche commodity is structured around a direct peg to a dominant benchmark, adjusted by an agreed-upon \textbf{quality} or \textbf{location spread}.

      Once the physical transaction is linked to the benchmark, the producer or consumer can execute an equal and opposite position in the financial futures market. This strategy creates a dual-market balancing mechanism: any subsequent loss in physical revenue caused by fluctuating spot prices is offset by a corresponding gain in the futures account, and vice versa. Ultimately, while this mechanism requires participants to \textbf{forgo the potential upside of favorable price movements}, it \textbf{secures operational certainty by locking in baseline profit margins}.

      \begin{enumerate}

        \item \textbf{Producer Hedging Against Brent Price Fall}

        A producer selling a discounted crude grade can contractually peg their sales price to \texttt{Brent - \$3.00/bbl} while simultaneously \textbf{shorting} Brent financial futures at \$75.00. If the market declines, the reduced revenue from the physical delivery at \$62.00 is precisely counterbalanced by a \$10.00 gain in the short position, thereby securing the targeted net effective return of \$72.00 per barrel.

        \item \textbf{Refiner Hedging Against Brent Price Rise}

        Conversely, a refiner purchasing a discounted crude grade can contractually peg their physical acquisition price to \texttt{Brent - \$3.00/bbl} while simultaneously executing a \textbf{long position} in Brent financial futures at \$75.00. If the market rallies, the increased procurement cost from the physical delivery at \$82.00 is precisely counterbalanced by a \$10.00 gain in the long position, thereby securing the targeted net effective purchase cost of \$72.00 per barrel.

      \end{enumerate}

    \begin{core}{Regional Markets and Arbitrage Dynamics}

      Crude oil does not trade at a singular, unified global price, operating instead through decentralized regional markets that rely on \textbf{localized benchmarks}. Under standard market conditions, the price differentials between these geographic boundaries remain fundamentally constrained; whenever localized imbalances widen these spreads beyond baseline transport costs, market participants exploit the resulting \textbf{arbitrage}, shifting physical supply and demand pressures until \textbf{prices realign}.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Investment Drivers and Market Financialization}

      As commodity markets have matured and gained liquidity, they have attracted a growing volume of purely financial participants who do not directly contribute to the physical value chain. This capital influx and participation in commodities markets are motivated by the following set of investment objectives.

      \begin{itemize}

        \item \textbf{Capital preservation} as a safe haven during periods of broader macroeconomic uncertainty, with tangible assets such as gold serving as a classic instrument for this purpose.

        \item \textbf{Portfolio diversification} through exposure to asset classes exhibiting historically low correlation with traditional securities such as equities and fixed income.

        \item \textbf{Speculative positioning} (\textit{\autoref{core:speculation}}) to capture profits from the inherent price volatility of commodities.

      \end{itemize}

      Like traditional financial assets, commodity spot prices are heavily responsive to shifts in broader market sentiment. This behavioral dynamic has become significantly more pronounced alongside the progressive \textbf{financialization of commodity markets} (\textit{\autoref{sec:fin_com}}), as maturing market liquidity has attracted a growing volume of purely financial participants who do not directly contribute to the physical value chain yet actively trade commodity contracts.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Price Drivers}

      The market valuation of primary commodities is dictated by a diverse array of structural, environmental, and institutional drivers.

      \begin{enumerate}

        \item \textbf{Weather \& Natural Disasters}

        Environmental conditions and severe weather anomalies exert direct, immediate shocks on regional commodity supply and demand. 

        \textit{For instance, an unseasonal cold wave in the United Kingdom can rapidly escalate residential heating requirements, triggering a sharp increase in regional natural gas prices due to the sudden demand surge.}

        \item \textbf{Geopolitical Events}

        Because no sovereign nation is entirely self-sufficient, global economic stability depends heavily on an interconnected network of commodity imports and exports. Consequently, any significant disruption to supply or demand dynamics within key trading nations can precipitate adverse cascading effects throughout the global economy.

        \textit{This is perfectly exemplified by the surge in crude oil prices stemming from the 2026 Iran-U.S.-Israel conflict (\autoref{fig:geopol_event}).}

        \item \textbf{Policy \& Regulation}

        Regulatory mandates and institutional policies frequently reshape demand configurations, directly affecting the profitability of refining and processing firms based on their specific technological and equipment constraints. 

        \myfig{1}{commodity_figures/CL=F_plot3}{Crude oil price response to escalating tensions associated with the U.S.-Israel-Iran conflict in March 2026}{fig:geopol_event}

        \textit{A prominent example is the International Maritime Organization (IMO) regulation mandating that all marine vessels utilize fuel with a sulfur content of 0.5\% or less, which rapidly altered refinery margins globally by shifting demand toward low-sulfur distillates.}

        \item \textbf{Demand Elasticity}

        Given that primary commodities function as essential societal \textbf{necessities}, aggregate consumer demand tends to be \textbf{relatively price-inelastic in the short term}, meaning that broader economic slowdowns typically manifest as \textbf{gradual, lagging shifts} rather than immediate disruptions. 

        There are, however, extreme exceptions to this trend. The outbreak of the \textbf{COVID-19 pandemic} rapidly decimated nearly one-third of global fuel demand. This unprecedented collapse in near-term consumption (\textit{\autoref{fig:clf2}}) forced the crude oil market into an extreme \textbf{super contango}, where immediate physical spot prices traded at a dramatic discount relative to longer-dated futures contracts.

      \end{enumerate}

    \end{core}

  \subsection{Financialization of Commodities}
    \label{sec:fin_com}

    \begin{core}{Historical Evolution}

      While physical supply and demand fundamentals remain foundational drivers of commodity valuation, broader market sentiment also exerts a significant influence on asset prices. This dynamic has intensified over the past few decades due to the progressive \textbf{financialization of the global oil market}, characterized by a substantial influx of non-traditional market participants actively trading energy derivatives.

      A prominent economic perspective suggests that the dramatic surge in oil prices between 2003 and 2008 (\textit{\autoref{fig:clf1}}) cannot be fully accounted for by physical supply and demand dynamics alone. Instead, this appreciation was heavily facilitated by the rapid financialization of oil futures markets---where trading volumes increased 20- to 30-fold and capital allocations expanded from less than \$10 billion to \$450 billion by April 2011---effectively transitioning these physical asset marketplaces into highly financialized arenas.

      \myfig{1}{commodity_figures/CL=F_plot1}{Crude oil future spot from Aug 2003 to Sep 2011}{fig:clf1}

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}[label=core:speculation]{Speculation \& May 2020 WTI Contract}

      Speculation involves the acquisition of assets at lower valuation baselines with the intent of selling at higher prices in the future. However, when aggressive speculative capital clashes with severe physical constraints, extreme structural market failures can occur. This vulnerability was vividly exposed during the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, when unprecedented global lockdowns triggered an immediate, massive collapse in oil consumption while physical crude production remained highly inelastic, forcing \textbf{millions of surplus barrels} into localized distribution networks.

      \myfig{1}{commodity_figures/CL=F_plot2}{WTI front-month futures prices from January 2020 to December 2020}{fig:clf2}

      As illustrated in \autoref{fig:clf2}, this supply-demand mismatch culminated in the historic \textbf{price collapse of the May 2020 WTI contract}. In the weeks preceding the crash, retail and institutional speculators aggressively channeled capital into crude oil ETFs and passive index products, attempting to capture the market bottom in anticipation of an economic rebound. Crucially, these financial participants overlooked the operational mechanics of the WTI contract, which mandates compulsory physical delivery upon expiration at Cushing, Oklahoma---the primary domestic pipeline crossroads with a working storage capacity of approximately 90 million barrels.

      On the eve of expiration, this massive influx of speculative capital encountered an absolute physical bottleneck. Because the global supply glut had filled Cushing's storage facilities to maximum capacity, non-physical market participants holding long positions faced a structural crisis. Lacking the infrastructure to accept physical delivery, these traders were legally required to roll their expiring May contracts into June by liquidating them at any cost. With a massive wall of forced sellers and zero available storage buyers, the market suffered a complete technical breakdown, ultimately driving prices into negative territory for the first time in history.

    \end{core}

\section{Commodity Players}

  \subsection{Producers \& Consumers}
    
    \begin{core}{Producers \& Consumers}

      Market participants within the commodity ecosystem are broadly classified into producers and consumers. \textbf{Producers} encompass upstream entities such as oil extraction firms and agricultural operations, whereas \textbf{consumers} include industrial manufacturing corporations and aviation firms that require physical raw materials for production.

      The strategic objectives of these two groups are inherently opposing: producers seek to secure the highest possible selling prices to protect revenues, while consumers aim to lock in the lowest feasible purchase prices to insulate margins. \textit{The aviation sector heavily relies on price locking via futures, as jet fuel procurement frequently accounts for 20\% to 50\% of total corporate operating costs.}

    \end{core}

  \subsection{Trading Houses}
    \label{sec:trading_houses}

    \begin{core}{Role as Intermediaries \& Vertical Integration}

      Direct transactions between primary producers and end consumers are relatively rare, with specialized \textbf{trading houses} typically intermediating these supply chains to manage the underlying logistics and import operations, alleviating the administrative and operational burdens on consuming firms.

      Rather than operating strictly as passive intermediaries, however, \textbf{modern trading houses frequently integrate vertically} by directly engaging in the extraction, refining, and processing of raw materials. Additionally, they leverage extensive logistical networks to source physical assets from a diversified global supplier base and distribute them efficiently to international end markets.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Arbitrage Strategies}

      Trading houses primarily capture profit margins by identifying and exploiting structural \textbf{market inefficiencies} through three principal \textbf{arbitrage} strategies. 

      \begin{enumerate}

        \item \textbf{Geographical arbitrage} involves purchasing a physical commodity at a lower price in one market and absorbing the freight costs to sell it at a premium in another location. 

        \item \textbf{Temporal arbitrage} entails acquiring inventory during periods of localized oversupply or low seasonal demand, managing its storage, and liquidating the asset when prices appreciate in the future. 

        \item \textbf{Production arbitrage} leverages processing capabilities, allowing firms to procure lower-cost raw materials and transform them into higher-value refined commodities.

      \end{enumerate}

    \end{core}

      As of June 2026, the global commodity intermediation landscape is highly consolidated, dominated by two principal groups of multinational trading conglomerates.

  \subsection{Investment Banks}

    \begin{core}{Investment Banks}

      Investment banks fulfill critical financial roles across the commodity value chain by providing essential capital and risk management solutions, such as structuring project financing, raising equity and debt capital, offering working capital solutions, and hedging physical exposure.

      Historically, many investment banks actively engaged in physical merchant trading and asset ownership. However, following the \textbf{2008 financial crisis}, regulatory reforms and legislative prohibitions compelled most Western institutions to divest from physical operations, though select banks in jurisdictions like Japan and Australia retain these physical capabilities.

    \end{core}

\section{Energy Markets}
  \label{sec:energy}

  \subsection{Crude Oil Fundamentals}

    \begin{core}{Brief History of Oil}

      Oil has been utilized at a large scale since approximately 6000 BC, with its commercial significance growing substantially following Abraham Gesner's invention of \textbf{kerosene} in 1852. Prior to the Industrial Revolution, agricultural commodities constituted the dominant class of traded goods; today, that position is held by \textbf{crude oil} (\textit{\autoref{core:crude}}). The scope of oil consumption is broad, spanning transportation fuels, industrial inputs, and the manufacturing of everyday household products.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}[label=core:crude]{Crude Oil \& Refineries}

      \textbf{Crude oil} is oil extracted from the ground in its \textbf{unprocessed state} that must undergo refining before it can be used commercially. The refinery process converts raw crude into a range of usable petroleum products, including diesel, jet fuel, and gasoline.

      \myfig{0.55}{commodity_figures/refinery}{Oil refinery}{fig:refinery}

      Refineries differ in both the specific mix or "recipe" of different crude oils they are capable of processing---collectively referred to as the \textbf{crude slate}---and the range of end products they can produce. More sophisticated refinery configurations can extract a greater number of refined products from a given barrel of crude. The profit generated from this conversion process is known as the \textbf{refinery margin}.

      Beyond the refining process itself, fundamentals such as storage capacity, transportation infrastructure, and quality differentials provide critical insight into the supply and demand structure of petroleum markets. Long-term demand for oil is forecast to grow, driven primarily by rising consumption in developing economies.

    \end{core}

    \subsection{Crude Oil Characteristics \& Types}

      \begin{core}[label=core:api_sulfur]{API Gravity \& Sulfur Content}

        Crude oil is a vast spectrum of hydrocarbons classified mainly by two physical traits.

        \begin{itemize}

          \item \textbf{API Gravity} measures the density of crude oil relative to water, a standard established by the \textbf{American Petroleum Institute (API)}. A higher API gravity denotes a \textbf{light} crude, which flows easily, yields valuable refined products, and commands a price premium over highly viscous \textbf{heavy} crudes.

          \item \textbf{Sulfur Content} dictates the refining complexity and environmental compliance costs of the oil. Oils with minimal sulfur concentrations are termed \textbf{sweet} crudes and are highly prized, whereas high-sulfur \textbf{sour} crudes require extensive, expensive hydrodesulfurization processes.

        \end{itemize}

        Beyond these primary metrics, a comprehensive chemical assay evaluates secondary physical traits such as \textbf{Total Acid Number (TAN)}, \textbf{heavy metal content}, \textbf{pour point}, \textbf{carbon residue}, \textbf{nitrogen content}, and \textbf{kinematic viscosity}. While it is important to remember that these characteristics heavily influence overall market value and refining logistics, a detailed exploration falls outside the scope of these notes.

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}{Crude Oil Types}

        As outlined in \autoref{core:api_sulfur}, crude oil produced across different fields varies considerably in its physical characteristics. Trading each stream individually would render global oil markets fragmented and operationally inefficient. To address this, the market has converged on a small number of benchmark crude grades---principally \textbf{Brent} (\textit{\autoref{core:brent}}), \textbf{WTI} (\textit{\autoref{core:wti}}), and \textbf{Dubai/Oman} (\textit{\autoref{core:oman}})---which serve as reference prices against which the vast majority of other crude streams are priced. Concentrating liquidity around these benchmarks achieves the standardization necessary for efficient global trade.

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}[label=core:brent]{Brent}

        \sbs{0.55}{
          Originating from extraction infrastructure across the \textbf{North Sea}, \textbf{Brent Crude} is technically a combination of several regional grades collectively known as the \textbf{BFOET blend} (\textit{Brent, Forties, Oseberg, Ekofisk, and Troll}). Classified as a \textbf{light, sweet} oil, it possesses a low density and minimal sulfur content, making it highly efficient for refining into premium transportation fuels.

          Due to being produced at offshore marine platforms, Brent is inherently a waterborne commodity, granting it exceptional \textbf{logistical flexibility for immediate global export}. This unparalleled market liquidity cements its role as the foremost global benchmark, effectively dictating the baseline valuation for approximately \textbf{60\% to 70\% of all internationally traded seaborne crude oil}.
        }{
          \myfig{0.84}{commodity_figures/north_sea}{North sea}{fig:north_sea}
        }

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}[label=core:wti]{Western Texas Intermediate}

        \sbs{0.45}{
          Produced in \textbf{North America}, \textbf{West Texas Intermediate} is characterized as exceptionally \textbf{light and sweet}, surpassing even Brent in quality. Predominantly extracted from the \textbf{Permian Basin}, \textbf{Eagle Ford Shale}, and \textbf{Bakken Formation}, it was historically confined to the domestic market, but following the \textbf{removal of export restrictions in 2015}, it has established itself as a highly influential global benchmark.
        }{
          \myfig{0.95}{commodity_figures/wti}{WTI crude extraction regions across the United States}{fig:wti}
        }

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}[label=core:oman]{Dubai/Oman}

        \sbs{0.5}{
          Extracted from the \textbf{Persian Gulf}, the \textbf{Dubai/Oman} benchmark is a pricing basket representing \textbf{medium, sour} crude oil. Characterized by a higher density and sulfur content, this blend anchors the pricing framework for the vast majority of \textbf{Middle Eastern crude oil} exports destined for markets \textbf{East of Suez}. Consequently, it serves as the fundamental reference price for highly complex refining hubs across Asia, predominantly within China, India, and Japan, which have specifically developed the infrastructure required to efficiently process these heavier, sulfur-rich crudes.
        }{
          \myfig{0.95}{commodity_figures/persian_gulf}{Persian gulf}{fig:persian_gulf}

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}{Other Crude Oil Types}

        When an oil does not match the three profiles discussed above, it still relies on the benchmarks for pricing, but the price differential fluctuates wildly based on transport logistics, refining difficulty, or geopolitics.

        \textbf{Western Canadian Select} (\textit{heavy Canadian crude}), for example, is landlocked, heavy, and extremely sour. It trades at a \textbf{massive discount to WTI} because refiners have to work much harder to process it. 

        \textbf{Russian Urals}, on the other hand, is a medium-sour blend and a prime example of a misfit oil whose status has been completely rewritten by geopolitics. Originally, Urals was the baseload crude for European refineries. Even though its physical profile was different, it was priced as a \textbf{discount to Brent} simply because it was sold into Brent's geographic territory. Following Western embargoes due to the \textbf{Ukraine invasion} (\textit{\autoref{core:ukraine}}), Russian oil was locked out of Europe and forced "East of Suez", finding new buyers in India and China. Because Urals is now flowing almost exclusively to Asia, and its physical properties are nearly identical to Dubai/Oman oil, it has detached from the Brent pricing sphere. It is \textbf{increasingly priced against the Dubai/Oman benchmark instead}.

      \end{core}

  \subsection{Supply Dynamics}

    \begin{core}[label=core:seven_sisters]{Seven Sisters \& Oil Majors}

      \sbs{0.5}{
        From the 1940s through to the 1970s, a group of seven American and British oil companies---collectively known as the \textbf{Seven Sisters}---exercised near-total dominance over global petroleum reserves, controlling approximately 85\% of the world's supply through vertical integration and cartel-like coordination. 

        Their monopolistic structure began to erode during the 1970s, driven by the growing assertion of national sovereignty over natural resources by oil-producing nations and the subsequent creation of \textbf{OPEC} (\textit{\autoref{core:opec}}) as an intergovernmental counterweight.
      }{
        \myfig{0.8}{commodity_figures/seven_sisters}{The Seven Sisters; \textit{Esso, Mobil, Chevron, Texaco, Gulf, Shell, British Petroleum/BP}}{fig:seven_sisters}
      }

      Of the Seven Sisters, four companies survive today---BP, Chevron, ExxonMobil (\textit{Esso}), and Royal Dutch Shell---and are collectively known as the \textbf{oil majors}, and the subsequent vacuum was filled by a set of \textbf{state-backed national oil companies}, often referred to as the \textbf{New Seven Sisters}. 

      \myfig{0.6}{commodity_figures/new_seven_sisters}{New Seven Sisters; \textit{Saudi Aramco (Saudi Arabia), Gazprom (Russia), CNPC (China), NIOC (Iran), PDVSA (Venezuela), Petrobras (Brazil), Petronas (Malaysia)}}{fig:new_seven_sisters}

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}[label=core:opec]{Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC)}

      The \textbf{Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC)}---an initial coalition of Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela---was established in September 14, 1960 during the \textbf{Baghdad conference} in Iraq with the primary objective of safeguarding sovereign revenues during the monopolistic reign of the Seven Sisters.

      In its early years, OPEC members sought to emulate the pricing practices of the oil majors by setting prices directly; this approach was subsequently replaced by market-based mechanisms, whereby member states influence prices indirectly through the adjustment of production levels.

      In the decades following its establishment, OPEC expanded its membership in response to key global events. These developments are discussed in detail in \autoref{sec:oil_history}.

      \myfig{0.5}{commodity_figures/bag_conf}{Baghdad conference on September 14, 1960}{fig:bag_conf}

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}[label=core:spr]{Strategic Petroleum Reserves}

      At the height of its influence, OPEC controlled approximately half of global crude oil production and held roughly 80\% of the world's proven reserves. Given the characteristically low price elasticity of demand for oil, this concentration of supply afforded the organization a substantial degree of market power.

      In response to the risks posed by this dependency, many consumer nations established \textbf{Strategic Petroleum Reserves (SPRs)}---government-controlled stockpiles of crude oil maintained to provide a buffer against supply disruptions and to safeguard national energy security. \textit{Japan's SPR was shown in \autoref{fig:japan_or}}.

    \end{core}

  \subsection{Historical Price Shocks and Events}
    \label{sec:oil_history}

    \subsubsection{The 1970s: Embargoes and Revolutions}

    \begin{core}[label=core:oil_embargo]{Arab Oil Embargo (1973)}

        In 1973, Arab oil-producing nations initiated an \textbf{export embargo} against the United States and its allies in retaliation for their military support of Israel during the \textbf{Yom Kippur War}. Concurrently, OPEC implemented \textbf{broader production cuts}, which caused \textbf{global oil prices to quadruple} rapidly.

        \myfig{0.85}{commodity_figures/embargo}{Crude oil prices from Feb 1971 to Mar 1977; \textit{1973 price spike due to the Arab oil embargo and broader production cuts}}{fig:embargo}

        While this unprecedented price surge initially generated massive revenue to OPEC member states, it catalyzed profound structural shifts that \textbf{ultimately led to severe demand destruction}. The price shock incentivized immediate countermeasures across major economies.

        The regulatory shift was mirrored in consumer and industry behavior, evidenced by a marked transition away from inefficient automobiles toward smaller, more fuel-efficient alternatives. Furthermore, the elevated price environment suddenly made capital-intensive exploration in non-OPEC regions---such as the North Sea and Alaska---economically viable, introducing substantial new competitive supply to the market. Confronted by this suppression of global oil consumption, OPEC adjusted its strategic approach, shifting its primary focus from maximizing short-term prices to maintaining long-term market stability.

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}[label=core:volcker]{Iranian Revolution \& The Volcker Shock (1979)}

        The Iranian Revolution in 1979 caused a severe disruption to the global oil supply as Iran's production and exports plummeted. This unexpected supply shock triggered a panic in the oil markets, leading to a rapid doubling of prices and significantly exacerbating the energy crisis of the 1970s. 

        \myfig{1}{commodity_figures/revolution}{Crude oil prices from May 1976 to Jul 1982; \textit{1979 price spike due to Iranian revolution}}{fig:revolution}

        The macroeconomic consequences were severe, pushing the global economy into a period of deep \textbf{stagflation}---a damaging combination of high inflation and stagnant economic growth. In the U.S., inflation reached double digits, prompting Federal Reserve Chairman Paul Volcker to intervene drastically in what became known as the \textbf{Volcker Shock}. By aggressively raising interest rates to unprecedented levels (\textit{peaking near 20 percent in 1981 as shown in \autoref{fig:volcker}}), Volcker successfully halted runaway inflation, but at the cost of triggering a harsh global recession. This resulting economic downturn ultimately crushed global demand for crude oil, setting the stage for the massive \textbf{price slump of the 1980s} (\textit{\autoref{core:price_slump}}).

        \myfig{1}{commodity_figures/DFF_plot1}{Federal funds rate from 1975 to 1984; \textit{roughly 20\% interest rates set by Federal Reserve Chairman Paul Volcker in response to the stagflation triggered by negative oil supply shock due to the Iranian Revolution of 1979}}{fig:volcker}

      \end{core}

    \subsubsection{The 1980s: Conflict and the Oil Glut}

      \begin{core}{Iran-Iraq War (1980)}

        In September 1980, Iraq invaded Iran in an attempt to capitalize on post-revolutionary instability and establish regional dominance. As both nations sought to cripple each other's war-funding capabilities, they systematically targeted opposing oil infrastructure, culminating in the "Tanker War" phase in the Persian Gulf. This mutual destruction initially removed millions of barrels of daily production capacity from the global market

        \myfig{0.75}{commodity_figures/iraq_war}{Iran-Iraq war}{fig:iraq_war}

        However, despite the massive scale of the conflict, the anticipated long-term oil shortage never materialized. The demand destruction initiated by the Volcker Shock (\textit{\autoref{core:volcker}}), coupled with aggressive production increases from Saudi Arabia and emerging non-OPEC suppliers effectively absorbed the negative supply shock from the Iran-Iraq War.

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}[label=core:price_slump]{Price Slump \& Market Restructuring (1980s)}

        Following the unprecedented price peaks of the 1970s, the 1980s were characterized by a \textbf{prolonged slump in global oil prices}, fundamentally driven by a structural realignment of both demand and supply.

        \begin{enumerate}

          \item \textbf{Demand Side Shifts}

          On the demand side, the elevated prices of the previous decade catalyzed widespread energy efficiency measures; industrialized nations implemented \textbf{strict vehicle fuel economy standards}, transitioned base-load power generation away from petroleum toward \textbf{nuclear and coal alternatives}, and began shifting their economies away from energy-intensive heavy manufacturing. 

          \item \textbf{Supply Side Consequences}

          Simultaneously, on the supply side, the artificially high prices maintained by OPEC heavily \textbf{incentivized capital-intensive exploration outside the cartel's control}. This resulted in a \textbf{massive influx of new, competitive crude oil} from emerging non-OPEC regions, most notably the technological triumphs in the \textbf{North Sea} and the \textbf{Alaskan North Slope}, alongside vast offshore discoveries in the \textbf{Cantarell Field} by Mexico and aggressive export maximization by the Soviet Union.

        \end{enumerate}

        This dual force of contracting global consumption and surging non-OPEC production systematically dismantled OPEC's pricing power, leading to a sustained supply glut that culminated in the dramatic \textbf{price collapse of 1986} (\textit{\autoref{core:price_collapse}}).

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}[label=core:price_collapse]{Price Collapse (1986)}

        By the mid-1980s, the global oil market was heavily saturated due to contracting demand and surging non-OPEC production. To defend high prices, OPEC relied heavily on Saudi Arabia to act as a \textbf{swing producer}, unilaterally cutting its own output to offset overproduction by other cartel members.

        \myfig{0.95}{commodity_figures/price_collapse}{Crude oil prices from Jan 1981 to Sep 1991; \textit{1986 price collapse due to sudden influx of Saudi crude via netback pricing}}{fig:price_collapse}

        However, after watching its market share and revenues plummet, Saudi Arabia fundamentally reversed its strategy in late 1985. Abandoning production quotas, the Kingdom aggressively increased production to reclaim market share and introduced \textbf{netback pricing}---a scheme tying crude costs directly to refined product prices, effectively guaranteeing buyer margins and undercutting competitors. This \textbf{sudden influx of Saudi crude} overwhelmed an already oversupplied market, causing global oil prices to crash from roughly \$27 per barrel to below \$10 per barrel in 1986 (\textit{\autoref{fig:price_collapse}}. This historic collapse devastated the economies of oil-exporting nations and decisively \textbf{ended the era of artificially sustained high prices}.

      \end{core}

    \subsubsection{The 1990s: Gulf War and Asian Demand Contraction}

      \begin{core}{Gulf War \& Operation Desert Storm (1990)}

        In August 1990, reeling from massive debts incurred during the Iran-Iraq War, Saddam Hussein ordered the Iraqi military to invade neighboring Kuwait. This prompted the United Nations to impose a \textbf{strict trade embargo}, abruptly removing approximately 4.3 million barrels per day of combined crude output from the global market. The subsequent supply disruption \textbf{triggered immediate market panic}, causing global oil prices to more than double by October. 

        \myfig{1}{commodity_figures/gulf_war}{Crude oil prices from Jan 1981 to Sep 1991; \textit{1990 price spike and plummet due to Gulf War and subsequent additional crude production by Saudi Aramco}}{fig:gulf_war}

        However, unlike the prolonged energy crises of the 1970s, this \textbf{price spike was remarkably short-lived}. To mitigate the macroeconomic fallout and cover the massive supply deficit, Saudi Aramco (\textit{a member of the New Seven Sisters mentioned in \autoref{core:seven_sisters}}) rapidly mobilized their spare capacity, significantly surging daily production. This decisive supply intervention, combined with the coordinated release of \textbf{strategic petroleum reserves} (\textit{\autoref{core:spr}}) by industrialized consumer nations and the swift military resolution of the conflict via \textbf{Operation Desert Storm}, successfully calmed the markets and drove oil prices back down to pre-invasion levels by early 1991.

        \myfig{0.6}{commodity_figures/jarhead}{\textit{Jarhead} (2005), a cinematic portrayal of U.S. Marine deployment during the 1990--1991 Gulf War}{fig:jarhead}

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}[label=core:aec]{Asian Financial Crisis (1997)}

        The \textbf{Asian Financial Crisis} of 1997 was triggered by the conversion of the Baht to float by the Thailand government, which consequently made foreign investors aggressively pull money out of the entire Asian market, realizing that Indonesia, South Korea, and Malaysia shared the same structural flaws as Thailand. This severely curtailed economic growth in the region, leading to a sharp decrease in the growth of global oil demand.

        Just as the crisis was unfolding in late 1997, OPEC held a ministerial \textbf{meeting in Jakarta}. Completely underestimating the severity and global reach of the Asian crisis, OPEC actually \textbf{agreed to increase its production quotas by 10\%} to capture what they believed was still a growing market.

        \myfig{1}{commodity_figures/aec}{Crude oil prices from Mar 1990 to Feb 2007; \textit{1997 V-shaped price pattern due to the Asian Economic Crisis and subsequent production and IMF corrections}}{fig:aec}

        Following the price collapse to roughly \$10 per barrel in late 1998, a swift response led to a market rebound. Recognizing their quota miscalculation, OPEC executed an emergency reversal in early 1999 by collaborating with major non-OPEC producers to implement global \textbf{production cuts by over two million barrels per day}. Concurrently, the affected Asian economies underwent severe but effective \textbf{IMF restructuring} programs. These mandated structural banking reforms and massive currency devaluations transformed the affected nations into highly competitive export engines, sparking a rapid V-shaped oil price pattern (\textit{\autoref{fig:aec}}). 

      \end{core}

    \subsubsection{The 2000s: The Asian Boom and the Global Financial Crisis}

      \begin{core}{The Dot-Com Crash, 9/11, and China (Early 2000s)}

        \sbs{0.5}{
          At the turn of the millennium, global oil markets experienced a brief but severe contraction driven by the bursting of the \textbf{Dot-Com bubble} and the profound negative demand shock following the \textbf{September 11, 2001 terrorist attacks on the Twin Towers}, which temporarily paralyzed the global aviation sector. While these events triggered a sharp decline in crude prices, the market rapidly stabilized through coordinated supply reductions by OPEC and key non-OPEC producers.
        }{
          \myfig{0.85}{commodity_figures/nine_eleven}{9/11 terrorist attacks on the Twin Towers in NYC}{fig:nine_eleven}
        }

        However, this temporary macroeconomic downturn masked a fundamental structural shift: China's official accession to the \textbf{World Trade Organization (WTO)}---an intergovernmental organization that regulates and facilitates international trade---in late 2001.

        \myfig{0.7}{commodity_figures/china_wto}{Signing ceremony for China's formal accession into the World Trade Organization}{fig:china_wto}

        China's subsequent industrial expansion provided a vital \textbf{demand floor during the Western recession}. As global economies recovered, \textbf{export-driven consumption} from China and other emerging markets ignited a massive commodity supercycle (\textit{eventually ended by the global financial crisis in 2008 as covered in \autoref{core:great_crash}}). The ensuing price escalation incentivized a new era of capital-intensive exploration and extraction activities.

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}[label=core:great_crash]{Global Financial Crisis (2008)}

        The \textbf{2008 global financial crisis}, triggered by the bursting of the \textbf{U.S. housing bubble}, caused a severe contraction in economic activity and a corresponding collapse in oil demand, driving prices from a record high of \$147 per barrel down to roughly \$32 per barrel in mere months.

        \myfig{1}{commodity_figures/great_crash}{Crude oil future spot from Aug 2003 to Sep 2011; \textit{oil price plummets in mid 2008 due to the financial crisis}}{fig:great_crash}

        To prevent a prolonged depression, global central banks implemented unprecedented monetary interventions, including \textbf{Zero Interest Rate Policies} and \textbf{Quantitative Easing}, while nations like China deployed massive fiscal stimulus aimed at heavy infrastructure. Concurrently, \textbf{OPEC cut production} to drain excess inventory. 

        This synchronized influx of global liquidity and aggressive stimulus successfully reignited crude demand and stabilized prices. Furthermore, this era of artificially cheap capital financed the development of hydraulic fracturing, igniting the \textbf{U.S. Shale Revolution} (\textit{\autoref{core:shale}}. As the global economy recovered, geopolitical fears that the ensuing \textbf{Arab Spring} unrest in North Africa (\textit{\autoref{core:arab_spring}}) and the Middle East might disrupt major oil-producing nations maintained persistent upward pressure on the rebounding market.

      \end{core}

    \subsubsection{The 2010s: Regional Instability, the U.S. Shale Boom, and Market Restructuring}

      \begin{core}[label=core:shale]{The U.S. Shale Revolution (2010s)}

        Following the \textbf{2008 financial crisis} (\textit{\autoref{core:great_crash}}), the unprecedented implementation of expansionary policy by the U.S. Federal Reserve initiated transformation in global energy markets. The resulting low interest rate environment allowed for the influx of exceptionally cheap capital to finance the proliferation of \textbf{hydraulic fracturing} and \textbf{horizontal drilling}---highly capital-intensive, short-cycle technologies that unlocked vast domestic shale reserves in the U.S., particularly in Texas, New Mexico, North Dakota, and Montana. Consequently, U.S. crude production surged, elevating the U.S. to the \textbf{world's largest oil producer} and \textbf{effectively dismantling OPEC's historical pricing monopoly}.

        \myfig{0.6}{commodity_figures/shale}{Shale oil pumpjacks}{fig:shale}

        This profound supply shock established a \textbf{persistent ceiling on global oil prices}, as short-cycle shale operations could rapidly scale production to cap oil market rallies. Furthermore, the shale boom precipitated the \textbf{2014--2016 market share price war} (\textit{\autoref{core:market_war}}) and permanently altered global macroeconomic trade flows by transitioning the United States into a dominant net exporter of petroleum.

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}[label=core:arab_spring]{The Arab Spring (2010--2011)}

        Commencing in late 2010, the \textbf{Arab Spring} was a widespread series of \textbf{anti-authoritarian uprisings} across the Middle East and North Africa, fundamentally driven by pervasive economic stagnation, political disenfranchisement, and acute food price shocks.

        \myfig{1}{commodity_figures/CL=F_plot5}{Crude oil future spot from Jan 2009 to Jan 2016; \textit{elevated crude price levels from 2011 to mid 2014 is due to the Arab Spring uprisings}}{fig:arab_spring}

        In the context of global energy markets, this severe regional instability introduced a substantial \textbf{geopolitical risk premium}. The most immediate physical supply disruption occurred during the \textbf{first Libyan civil war} in 2011, which abruptly removed approximately 1.5 million barrels per day of premium light, sweet crude from the global market. This distinct supply shock, compounded by pervasive fears that the political contagion might \textbf{destabilize major Gulf state producers}, drove global oil prices back above \$100 per barrel.

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}[label=core:market_war]{Market Share War \& OPEC+ (2014--2016)}

        The sustained elevated oil prices resulting from the geopolitical risk premium of the Arab Spring inadvertently subsidized the capital-intensive expansion of the \textbf{U.S. shale industry} (\textit{\autoref{core:shale}}). By 2014, the exponential growth of U.S. tight oil production precipitated a severe global supply glut. Rather than relinquishing market share through traditional production cuts, OPEC, guided by Saudi Arabia, initiated a deliberate market share war by maintaining high output to collapse prices and financially ruin U.S. shale producers.

        \myfig{0.75}{commodity_figures/doc}{Declaration of Cooperation between OPEC and non-OPEC nations}{fig:doc}

        However, the technological resilience and rapid cost-efficiency improvements of the shale sector thwarted this objective, instead inflicting severe economic distress upon traditional petrostates. Acknowledging that the \textbf{traditional cartel's diminished global market share} precluded unilateral price manipulation, OPEC and key non-OPEC producers, \textbf{predominantly Russia}, ratified the \textbf{Declaration of Cooperation} in late 2016. This historic strategic alliance established \textbf{OPEC+}, a super-cartel commanding over half of global production, thereby regaining the necessary leverage to coordinate supply reductions and restore structural stability to the market.

      \end{core}

    \subsubsection{The 2020s: Pandemics and Geopolitical Conflicts}

      \begin{core}[label=core:pandemic]{The Saudi-Russia Price War \& COVID-19 Pandemic (2020)}

        In early March 2020, as the initial disruptions of the \textbf{COVID-19 pandemic began eroding global energy demand}, OPEC+ convened in Vienna to negotiate a stabilizing response. Saudi Arabia, leading the traditional OPEC coalition, proposed a collective production cut of 1.5 million barrels per day to offset the looming consumption deficit and prevent a severe price contraction. \textbf{Russia, the primary non-OPEC partner, firmly rejected the proposal}, fearing that further supply reductions would only sacrifice Russian market share to U.S. shale producers, who had historically capitalized on OPEC+ price floors to expand their own operations.

        Interpreting Russia's refusal as a strategic betrayal, Saudi Arabia immediately pivoted from a policy of price stabilization to \textbf{market share maximization}. Within 48 hours of the collapsed negotiations, Riyadh (\textit{the capital of Saudi Arabia}) aggressively slashed its crude export prices across all major global markets, offering the deepest discount rates seen in decades. Simultaneously, Saudi Aramco announced a deliberate expansion of its output, drawing on its spare capacity to \textbf{push production to a maximum} of over 12 million barrels per day.

        \myfig{1}{commodity_figures/CL=F_plot6}{Crude oil future spot from Jan 2019 to Jan 2023; \textit{crude prices dip in early 2020 due to the positive supply shock from the Saudi-Russia price war and diminished demand due to the pandemic}}{fig:pandemic}

        This punitive retaliation was explicitly designed to flood the global market, crash crude prices, and inflict acute financial distress on the Russian economy and higher-cost U.S. shale operators. The resulting price war unfolded at the same time as global oil demand was collapsing due to the COVID-19 pandemic---a combination of shocks that gave rise to severe market dislocations, most notably the \textbf{May 2020 WTI futures contract} incident discussed in \textit{\autoref{core:speculation}}.

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}[label=core:ukraine]{The Russia-Ukraine War (2022)}

        The 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine fundamentally ruptured global energy markets, weaponizing petroleum flows and prompting unprecedented \textbf{Western sanctions}, including seaborne embargoes and a price cap mechanism implemented by the G7, an alliance of seven major advanced economies (\textit{Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom, and the United States}).

        Consequently, global trade routes underwent a massive structural realignment; Russia was forced to reroute its heavily discounted crude eastward to Asian markets utilizing a \textbf{shadow fleet}, while Europe severed its historical dependence on Moscow by rapidly \textbf{pivoting to U.S. crude and liquefied natural gas (LNG)}. This permanent logistical shift cemented American energy dominance and simultaneously deepened Russia's strategic reliance on the OPEC+ cartel to sustain its economically isolated war effort through coordinated price stabilization.

        \textit{As of June 5, 2026, this war is still ongoing.}

        \myfig{0.95}{commodity_figures/CL=F_plot7}{Crude oil future spot from Jan 2021 to Jan 2024; \textit{crude prices spike in early 2022 due to the supply disruption caused by the Russian invasion of Ukraine}}{fig:ukraine}

      \end{core}

      \begin{core}[label=core:hormuz_crisis_2026]{Strait of Hormuz Crisis (2026)}

        The 2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis commenced in late February following U.S. and Israeli airstrikes on Iran, prompting Tehran to retaliate by effectively closing the critical maritime chokepoint on February 28. This closure triggered an unprecedented scale of disruption, as the strait historically facilitated roughly 20\% of global oil trade. The conflict removed approximately 14\% of projected global supply from the market and severely threatening energy-dependent Asian economies.

        \myfig{0.95}{commodity_figures/CL=F_plot8}{Crude oil future spot from Jan 2024 to Jun 2026; \textit{crude prices spike in February 2026 due to the Israel-Iran-U.S. conflict and the consequent closing of the Strait of Hormuz}}{fig:strait}

        The most immediate consequence for global oil markets was a sharp upward spike in crude prices. In response, the \textbf{International Energy Agency (IEA)} coordinated an emergency \href{https://www.iea.org/news/iea-member-countries-to-carry-out-largest-ever-oil-stock-release-amid-market-disruptions-from-middle-east-conflict}{release of 400 million barrels} from member country reserves---the largest such intervention in the organization's history. Beyond the crude market, the crisis exerted significant upward pressure on the prices of heavier refined products, and is widely regarded as a key contributor to the sharp acceleration in consumer price inflation, with the U.S. CPI rising on a year-over-year basis from 2.4\% in February to 3.8\% in April.

        \textit{As of June 5th, 2026, the conflict remains ongoing, although negotiations toward a ceasefire and a broader peace agreement are actively underway.}

      \end{core}
