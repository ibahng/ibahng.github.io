\section{Market Foundation}

  \subsection{Equities \& The Stock Market}

    \begin{core}{Equity}
      % Equities, stocks, and shares all mean the same thing—part ownership of a company
      Equities, stocks, and shares are interchangeable terms representing fractional ownership stakes in a corporate entity. Holding equity vests an investor with a proportional claim on the underlying firm's net assets and ongoing financial distributions, functioning as the primary asset class for long-term capital appreciation and corporate governance participation.
    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{The Stock Market}
      % The stock market's purpose is to allocate resources to productive enterprises. The stock exchange is appealing for two reasons:
      % * Stock market makes it easy to enter and exit company investments. A private investor can quickly and easily own parts of thousands of companies
      % * Picking the right company could earn you millions
      % Stocks go up and down throughout the trading day. Most US stocks trade from 9:30 AM to 4:00 PM, New York Time (after hours also exist as well).
      The fundamental economic purpose of the stock market is to efficiently allocate capital to productive commercial enterprises while providing secondary market liquidity. Public exchanges lower barriers to entry, enabling retail and institutional investors to easily diversify risk by holding fractional stakes across thousands of companies, with the speculative upside of asymmetric financial returns. Asset prices fluctuate continuously throughout the standardized trading session—which in the United States spans from 9:30 AM to 4:00 PM Eastern Time—supplemented by extended pre-market and after-hours electronic networks.
    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{IPOs and Delistings}
      % Companies typically go public to raise money to expand the business or enable founders or owners to sell their stakes to the public, or both. Going public is down through Initial Public Offerings or IPOs—which are managed between the company and an investment bank.
      % While equities list in splashy IPOs, they tend to delist more discreetly. They can delist for many reasons:
      % * The company may have been bought by a private equity fund
      % * Management may have been fed up w/ the regulatory burden of being public
      % * The company may have gone bust
      % * A whole industry may have died (EX. Kodak)
      % * There are also good reasons, such as successful mergers/acquisitions
      Corporations transition from private to public status via an Initial Public Offering (IPO), an underwriting process managed by investment banks designed to raise expansionary capital, optimize balance sheet structures, or allow early founders to monetize their stakes. Conversely, companies delist from public exchanges under less publicized circumstances. Delisting triggers include strategic take-private acquisitions by private equity funds, corporate restructuring to eliminate public regulatory burdens, corporate insolvency, or secular industry obsolescence (e.g., Kodak). Alternatively, benign delistings regularly occur through value-enhancing corporate mergers and acquisitions.
    \end{core}

  \subsection{Indices}

    \begin{core}{Equity Indices}
      % Indices are baskets of stocks.
      % Equity indices are useful not only for monitoring market movements. They can also tell whether a money manager has beaten the market.
      % Indices are diverse:
      % EX. Broken down by region
      % However, while an index may be domiciled in one country, companies therein may have revenues coming from many other countries.
      % Company size, industry, and country are the three most common organizing principles for stock market indices.
      % 	EX. S&P 500 membership → size (MKT CAP)
      % Size in the S&P 500 tells you how much it also contributes towards the moves in the index.
      % 	EX. Apple MKT CAP = $724.89B; S&P 500 = $18.2T
      % Index Weight % = ($724.89B / $18.2T) * 100% = 3.98%
      An equity index is a curated basket of stocks serving as a macroeconomic barometer and a performance benchmark to evaluate whether active investment managers are outperforming the market. Indices are constructed using three primary organizing dimensions: capitalization size, industry sector, or geographic domicile, though indexed multinational corporations often derive their underlying revenues globally rather than from their home country. In a market-capitalization-weighted index like the S&P 500, a firm's total market value dictates its structural index configuration and relative contribution to daily index point movements. For example, if a firm possesses a market capitalization of \$724.89 billion within an aggregate index valuation of \$18.2 trillion, its mathematical influence is expressed as:
      $$\text{Index Weight \%} = \left( \frac{\$724.89\text{B}}{\$18.2\text{T}} \right) \times 100\% = 3.98\%$$
    \end{core}

  \subsection{Funds}

    \begin{core}{Mutual Funds}
      Mutual funds are pooled investment vehicles that aggregate capital from retail and institutional investors to purchase a diversified portfolio of securities aligned with a specific mandate. Managed by professional asset management firms, traditional mutual funds calculate their Net Asset Value (NAV) once per day at the market close, restricting entries and exits to end-of-day transactions while often charging active management fees or sales loads.
    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs)}
      Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs) are investment vehicles holding baskets of underlying assets that replicate indices, commodity baskets, or specific factor strategies. Unlike traditional mutual funds, ETFs trade intraday on open public exchanges like individual stocks, leveraging a specialized institutional creation-redemption mechanism managed by Authorized Participants (APs) to keep the secondary market share price closely aligned with the fund's underlying intrinsic value.
    \end{core}

\section{Equity Fundamentals}

  \subsection{The Nature of Equities}

    \begin{core}{Residual Claims}
      % An equity promises residual cash flows from a company—that is, what is left when all other claims are satisfied.
      % Stocks are high risk, high return assets while bonds are low risk, low return. Closer to retirement usually entails a larger ownership of bonds relative to stocks.
      % With decades worth of data, we can clearly see that the bond market has been much less volatile than the stock market.
      Equity securities structurally represent a residual claim on a corporation's financial resources, meaning shareholders are entitled only to the remaining net cash flows and assets after all senior obligations—such as employee wages, taxes, supplier invoices, and debt service—are fully settled. Consequently, equities operate as high-risk, high-return assets exhibiting greater long-term return volatility than fixed-income instruments. This volatility necessitates a life-cycle asset allocation shift, where investors gradually increase low-risk bond allocations relative to equities as they approach retirement to preserve accumulated capital.
    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Stock vs Bond Returns}
      % Factors to consider when comparing stock/bond returns:
      % * Returns from dividends: The reinvestments of dividends into the index fund viewed can entail a massive difference when magnified over several years. This is the main distinguisher that makes equities far more attractive than bonds.
      % * The nominal nature of stock and bond returns: Inflation eats up returns for both types of securities; when comparing returns to GDP, make sure to compare with nominal GDP.
      % Bond downsides and upsides are both finite while stock upsides are infinite and downsides are only finite.
      When analyzing the performance differential between equities and fixed income, compounding dividend reinvestment serves as the primary mechanism amplifying equity returns over multi-year horizons, distinguishing stocks from fixed-coupon bonds. Both asset classes yield nominal returns vulnerable to purchasing power erosion from inflation; thus, performance benchmarking against macroeconomic output must utilize nominal GDP figures. Structurally, the payoff profile of a bond is bounded on both sides, whereas an equity position exhibits a finite downside restricted to the initial principal and a theoretically infinite upside driven by corporate growth.
    \end{core}

  \subsection{Shareholder Owndership}

    \begin{core}{Ownership Structure}
      % What do shareholders actually own:
      % * The earnings that a company generates after paying of all of its expenses
      % * The sum total of company assets that are not owed to lenders—"net assets"
      Shareholder ownership comprises two core balance sheet elements: a legal claim on net earnings generated after all operating and non-operating expenses are deducted, and a proportional right to the corporation's net assets (total assets minus total liabilities). This structural positioning designates equity holders as the ultimate economic beneficiaries of operational scale, intellectual property expansion, and corporate equity accumulation.
    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Earnings Volatility}
      % Earnings are a stream of future cash flow just like bond repayments are a stream of future cash flows. However, fixed income repayments are preordained, whereas equity earnings are not. Changes in revenues and costs make residual earnings volatile.
      Corporate earnings represent a stream of projected future cash flows conceptually analogous to the contractual interest and principal repayments of fixed-income instruments. However, while debt cash flows are legally preordained and contractually fixed, equity cash flows remain unconstrained and structurally uncertain. Continuous variations in corporate revenue streams and input cost bases render residual corporate earnings highly volatile, directly exposing equity valuations to macroeconomic and operational shifts.
    \end{core}

\section{Equity Analysis}

  \subsection{Equity Research}

    \begin{core}{Revenue \& Earnings Analysis}
      % How big is the pie and how big is the company's slice of that pie?
      % The more successful a product is against its competitors, the larger chunk of market share it receives (the bigger slice).
      % Quarterly financial numbers are reported to investors at earnings announcements.
      % While most quarterly financial releases align with the calendar year, many companies also don't. However here is the typical date range for the quarterly financial releases (earnings season):
      % * Q1: Mid April
      % * Q2: Late July
      % * Q3: Late October
      % * Q4: Early February
      % How to estimate future company earnings:
      % * Industry Classification
      % * Suppliers and Buyers
      % * Revenue Projections
      % * Cost Base
      % earnings drivers
      % The world is full of surprising underlying drivers:
      % * Pandemics affect the number of air travelers
      % * High Wall Street bonuses boost jewelry sales
      % * Booming stock markets lift recreational vehicle sales etc.
      % Investing analysts estimate upcoming earnings by examining industry drivers and seeing their relative impact on earnings and comparing it to other analysts' estimates.
      Fundamental equity research evaluates a firm's operational efficacy by analyzing its Total Addressable Market (TAM) and competitive market share. Corporate performance data is reported during highly synchronized quarterly earnings seasons; while fiscal calendars vary by firm, these corporate announcements typically cluster in mid-April (Q1), late July (Q2), late October (Q3), and early February (Q4). Financial analysts model future corporate earnings profiles by auditing industry hierarchies, supply-chain bargaining dynamics (suppliers versus buyers), revenue growth vectors, and underlying fixed-and-variable cost structures. This modeling incorporates disparate macroeconomic drivers—such as public health disruptions affecting global passenger aviation metrics, financial sector bonus pools steering luxury goods demand, or capital market returns influencing high-end recreational vehicle sales—to calculate variant estimates against consensus Wall Street projections.
    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Earnings Beat vs Miss}
      % When the actual results are above the estimates, it is called a "beat" and when below, a "miss". When a company "beats" or "misses" tends to be in the headline of an earnings news story.
      % Analysts are not infallible. They often struggle to spot turning points—which companies can use to beat estimates several quarters in a row.
      % While earnings estimate beats usually lead to a rise in share price and a miss leads to a fall, sometimes this relationship doesn't hold off.
      % There is more to guessing share price movements than accurately estimating earnings. It's called valuation.
      When a corporation reports quarterly financial figures exceeding Wall Street consensus expectations, the event is categorized as an earnings "beat," whereas underperformance constitutes an earnings "miss." Because forecasting models struggle to identify structural macroeconomic turning points or hidden operational efficiencies, corporations frequently engineering consecutive quarters of earnings beats. Although an earnings beat typically acts as a bullish catalyst for share prices and a miss triggers selling, this directional correlation can decouple based on market positioning, forward guidance, and underlying asset valuation metrics.
    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Industry Classification}
      % Same firms operate in only one or several industries.
      % Industries are arranged into hierarchies. In the S&P 500, they are arranged into 11 big industry families. 
      % When examining a company's revenue, it is best to distinguish it into distinct industries.
      % suppliers and buyers
      % All companies exist in an ecosystem of suppliers, competitors, and buyers.
      % Understanding the web of relationships within this supply chain can lead to more accurate estimates.
      % Dividing the costs of different parts of a product gives insight into how cost changes may impact product earnings largely or miniscule.
      % all originate fundamentally from commodities and are usually different compositions across industries
      Corporations routinely deploy capital across multiple distinct, overlapping business lines, requiring analysts to decompose aggregate corporate revenue structures into specific industry segments. In major public benchmarks like the S&P 500, these segments are organized according to standardized taxonomies like the Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS), which divides the market into 11 broad sectors. Equity researchers evaluate these sectors by mapping out corporate ecosystems consisting of suppliers, direct competitors, and downstream buyers. Deconstructing product bill-of-materials and industrial input costs reveals how margin profiles respond to primary commodity price fluctuations, which serve as the foundational baseline across all global supply chains.
    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Case Study (\textit{Rolls-Royce})}
      % Most of RR's revenue comes from selling aircraft engines. From 2003 to 2014, revenue rose from 6B to 14B pounds.
      % This is because aircraft manufacturers did well because airlines did well because of increased plane ticket demand.
      % Once this web is mapped out, an analyst looks at historic performance using reported financial number to estimate earnings (Earnings statement, investor conference calls' transcripts).
      The industrial scaling of Rolls-Royce from 2003 to 2014 provides a clear case study in supply-chain integration, as its core aviation engine revenues expanded from £6 billion to £14 billion. This top-line expansion was driven by a linear macroeconomic transmission mechanism: accelerating consumer demand for commercial aviation boosted airline profitability, increasing long-term capital orders for commercial aircraft manufacturers, which passed directly through to engine suppliers. To model these structural dependencies, analysts evaluate audited income statements, regulatory disclosures, and executive conference call transcripts to map historical baseline variables into future cash flow forecasts.
    \end{core}

  \subsection{Valuation}

    \begin{core}{Market Capitalization}
      % In the US, 3% of all stocks change hands each business day.
      % The number of shares a company multiplied by the price per share gives you the size of the company, otherwise known as the market capitalization, the main factor investors care about when valuing a company (do not compare share price to one another).
      Market capitalization represents the total aggregate dollar value of a public company's outstanding equity, calculated by multiplying the total number of shares outstanding by the current market price per share. In the highly liquid United States equity market, approximately 3\% of total outstanding equity changes hands during an average business day. Market capitalization serves as the primary gauge of corporate scale for institutional investors, rendering direct comparisons between raw per-share stock prices fundamentally meaningless without reference to total shares outstanding.
    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Corporate Actions (\textit{Stock Splits and Buybacks})}
      % A stock split is when a company feels that its share price is too high for retail investors, so it divides into two or more shares.
      % A reverse stock split, also known as a stock consolidation, it the opposite.
      % When a company thinks its own stock is sheep, it might explore another corporate action: stock buybacks. When a company does a stock buyback, the value of each stock remaining in the market goes up because there are now fewer slices of bread (supply and demand).
      When a corporation determines its high per-share price restricts liquidity among retail market participants, it executes a forward stock split, proportionally dividing existing shares into multiple new units to lower the nominal price per share without altering the total underlying equity value. Conversely, a reverse stock split consolidates outstanding share volume to raise the nominal stock price, often to meet exchange listing requirements. When an undervalued corporation possesses excess balance-sheet liquidity, it can launch a share buyback program; by purchasing and retiring its own stock, the firm reduces the aggregate share float, concentrating residual earnings power across fewer outstanding shares to drive up intrinsic per-share value.
    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Share Prices vs. Valuations}
      % Share prices are facts while valuations are opinion. Remember that equity holders own residual cash flow, thus they care deeply about earnings estimates.
      % Analysts tend to change their earnings estimates for a company after quarterly earnings announcements and after any other news that forces a rethink of the company's prospects.
      A fundamental axiom of equity research dictates that market share prices represent verified transactional facts, whereas financial valuations represent dynamic analytical opinions. Because equity ownership targets residual corporate cash flows, valuation models are highly sensitive to adjustments in long-term earnings projections. Analysts systematically adjust their forecasting variables immediately following quarterly earnings releases, macroeconomic data shifts, or corporate updates that reshape a firm's long-term growth trajectory.
    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Absolute (\textit{Intrinsic}) vs. Relative Valuation}
      % There are two common ways in which investors value companies:
      % * Absolute Valuation is when an analyst estimates future earnings and then, in turn, estimates how much those estimates are worth in today's money.
      % * Relative Valuation is when an analyst compares a company to another similar company or to the overall market to define relative value.
      % Absolute Valuation vs Relative Valuation
      % ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
      % Feature | Absolute Valuation | Relative Valuation
      % ---|---|---
      % Pros | Precise, Anchored to earnings, Disciplined thought process | Easier to understand, Simple to calculate, Does not demand long-term functions
      % Cons | Demands clairvoyance, Laborious, Prone to subtle manipulation | Directional, Hard to find truly comparable firms, Presupposes that the company you are comparing it to is fairly valued
      % absolute = dcf
      % relative = comps
      % EXTERNAL REFERENCE TO VALUATION
      Equity asset pricing relies on two primary methodologies: Absolute (Intrinsic) Valuation, which models long-term cash flows and discounts them to present value via a Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) architecture, and Relative Valuation, which appraises corporate value by benchmarking financial ratios against a peer group of comparable companies (Comps).

    \begin{core}{Economic Linkages}
      % Recall that earnings press conferences are released weeks after their relevant period, thus an increase in market value of an index is simply a reaction to the announcement of earnings increase or decrease.
      % Economic Indicators -> Real GDP Growth -> Corporate Earnings -> Equities
      % P/E ratios are subject to the "reversion to the mean", however this law breaks down when we consider corporate earnings because they are linked to the economy which has grown consistently since the industrial revolution.
      Because corporate earnings reports are backward-looking disclosures released weeks after the conclusion of a fiscal period, immediate equity index re-pricings reflect structural market updates to forward earnings estimates. Equity asset returns trace a clear macroeconomic transmission line:
      $$\text{Economic Indicators} \longrightarrow \text{Real GDP Growth} \longrightarrow \text{Corporate Earnings} \longrightarrow \text{Equity Valuations}$$
      While market valuation multiples like Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratios are historically bound by financial mean reversion, this limiting law does not restrict nominal corporate earnings over long horizons, as they remain structurally linked to an expanding global economy that has scaled continuously since the Industrial Revolution.
    \end{core}

\section{Historical Events}

  \subsection{The Great Crash}

    \begin{core}{The Great Crash of 1929}

      % Following the speculative mania of the "Roaring Twenties"---which was propelled by rapid industrial electrification, mass retail adoption of unhedged margin account leverage, and unregulated brokerage pools---the U.S. stock market peaked in September 1929 and violently unwound. On Black Tuesday (October 29, 1929), heavy panic liquidation overwhelmed the open-outcry floor of the New York Stock Exchange, causing the Dow Jones Industrial Average to crater 12\% in a single trading session, ultimately destroying nearly 90\% of aggregate public equity value by its 1932 cyclical trough. This systemic destruction of household and corporate wealth permanently altered the U.S. financial system, forcing Congress to eliminate laissez-faire capital market policies by passing the Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, which established the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and mandated audited corporate balance sheet disclosures.

    \end{core}

  \subsection{The Electronic \& Programmatic Turn}

    \begin{core}{The Inception of NASDAQ \& Electronic Quotation (1971)}

      % On February 8, 1971, the National Association of Securities Dealers automated its quotation systems, officially launching the NASDAQ as the world's first purely electronic stock market. Initially operating as a computerized bulletin board displaying real-time, non-negotiable bid and ask quotes from fragmented market makers, the system fundamentally disintermediated the traditional monopoly held by specialist-driven, physical open-outcry trading floors like the New York Stock Exchange. By consolidating price transparency onto digital screens across the country, NASDAQ dramatically tightened transaction bid-ask spreads, laid the structural technical groundwork for modern automated routing engines and high-frequency execution networks, and established a specialized listing home that fueled the growth of the burgeoning global technology sector.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Black Monday and the Advent of Portfolio Insurance (1987)}

      % On October 19, 1987, the Dow Jones Industrial Average plummeted by 22.6\% in a single trading session, marking the largest single-day percentage decline in modern equity history. The crash was catalyzed by a fatal systemic feedback loop involving an early quantitative hedging strategy known as \textbf{Portfolio Insurance}, which used algorithmic computer programs to automatically short S&P 500 futures contracts when cash equity prices dropped. When early selling hit the market, these automated execution strategies triggered massive, non-economic futures shorting, which dragged down the cash equity index further, creating a cascading avalanche of programmatic liquidation that completely exhausted institutional market-maker capital. In response to this operational black swan, global exchanges implemented mandatory, market-wide programmatic circuit breakers to temporarily halt asset trading during extreme intraday point drops to allow liquidity providers to re-price risk book structures.

    \end{core}

  \subsection{Modern Speculative Manias \& Microstructure Shocks}

    \begin{core}{The Dot-Com Meltdown (2000)}

      % The Dot-Com crisis represents a premier modern example of a speculative technology bubble driven by extreme valuation distortions and non-traditional market metrics. Throughout the late 1990s, the commercialization of the internet triggered massive capital flows into any early-stage enterprise deploying a ".com" suffix, driving the trailing Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio of the NASDAQ Composite Index past 100 as investors prioritized website traffic eye-balls and click-through rates over net income. The structural mismatch between speculative valuations and actual cash-flow realities triggered a violent unwinding in March 2000, wiping out \$5 trillion in public equity wealth over the ensuing two years, bankrupting heavily capitalized corporate shells, and forcing institutional research analysts to return to rigid fundamental free-cash-flow modeling and capital discipline.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{The High-Frequency Flash Crash (2010)}

      % On May 6, 2010, the major U.S. equity benchmarks suffered an unprecedented, near-instantaneous intraday collapse, with the Dow Jones dropping roughly 1,000 points (9\%) in less than 36 minutes before recovering the vast majority of its losses before the closing bell. The event was driven by the operational dynamics of a fragmented, high-frequency trading (HFT) market microstructure, where a massive algorithmic sell order in the E-mini S&P 500 futures market triggered automated execution strings. Realizing that systemic volatility was spiking non-fundamentally, automated HFT market-making algorithms simultaneously pulled their bids from the electronic order book, creating a momentary liquidity vacuum where equity shares traded at absurd prices. This structural fracture forced regulators to implement localized, stock-specific \textbf{Limit-Up/Limit-Down (LULD) bands} to prevent automated routing engines from bypassing baseline market liquidity.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{The Meme Stock Short Squeeze \& Gamma Squeezes (2021)}

      % The GameStop (GME) short squeeze of January 2021 exposed a major paradigm shift in modern market microstructure, where crowdsourced retail capital directly weaponized derivative plumbing to force institutional liquidations. Utilizing zero-commission brokerage apps and social media forums (such as Reddit's r/wallstreetbets), millions of retail traders coordinated massive, aggregate purchases of deep out-of-the-money call options on heavily shorted equities. This buying behavior triggered an intense \textbf{Gamma Squeeze}: as call option volumes surged, institutional market makers were legally forced to continuously purchase the underlying physical shares to maintain a delta-neutral options book. This forced hedging loop work in tandem with a traditional short squeeze, driving GameStop's equity up over 1,700\% in weeks, inflicting multi-billion-dollar losses on institutional short-selling hedge funds, and forcing clearinghouses to restrict brokerage transaction capacity due to soaring collateral margin mandates.

    \end{core}
