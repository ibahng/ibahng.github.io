\section{Market Foundation}

  \subsection{The Global Bond Market}

    \begin{core}{Market Size \& Composition}

      % The fixed-income market is the largest market in the world. At the end of 2016, there were over $101T worth of traded bonds in existence worldwide.
      % GDP was $79T at the time.
      % World stock market was $65T.
      The fixed-income market represents the largest financial market globally. By the end of 2016, the total value of traded bonds worldwide exceeded \$101 trillion, surpassing both global GDP (\$79 trillion) and total global equity market capitalization (\$65 trillion). 

      % There are 2.1M government and corporate bonds in existence (adds up to $101T).
      % Corporate bonds compose 1.6M of the 2.1M bonds outstanding.
      % There is a greater diversity in corporate bonds, and like the US government, a single corporation can also issue a vast array of bonds.
      This vast market comprised approximately 2.1 million distinct government and corporate bonds. Corporate bonds accounted for the majority, with roughly 1.6 million outstanding issues, reflecting the immense diversity of corporate debt instruments. 

      % The US treasury had 1086 actively traded bonds and bills outstanding (accounting for $14T).
      % The US bond market plays a crucial role in global finance.
      % US corporate bonds account for $8T of the market (the 2nd largest segment).
      % The US bond market's relative size is one of the main reasons for dollar centricity in the currency market.
      The United States Treasury market, while consisting of only 1,086 actively traded bonds and bills, represented \$14 trillion in value. Furthermore, the US corporate bond sector constituted the second-largest segment at \$8 trillion. The sheer scale and liquidity of the US bond market are primary drivers of the US dollar's central role in global currency markets.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Historical Development}

      % The bond market is relatively recent like the FX market, born out of:
      % * IR liberalization
      % * Removal of capital controls after WWII, making it easier for governments to borrow

      % Governments used to raise money for a few cases such as war and justice. But with the advent of the welfare state and social safety net, government spending as a proportion of GDP has risen steeply as advanced nations now pay more for healthcare, education, and other public services.

      The modern bond market, much like the foreign exchange market, is a relatively recent development. Its expansion was primarily driven by interest rate liberalization and the dismantling of capital controls following World War II, which greatly facilitated sovereign borrowing. Historically, governments primarily issued debt to finance extraordinary expenses such as wars and the administration of justice. However, the rise of the modern welfare state has fundamentally shifted this dynamic. As developed nations expanded public services, including healthcare, education, and social safety nets, government spending as a proportion of GDP increased significantly, leading to a corresponding structural reliance on debt financing.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{US Treasuries \& Foreign Nations}

      % Since currencies floated freely since 1971, many countries, have been forced to accumulate war chests of FX reserves to defend their pegs.
      % Other countries sell their own currencies to buy US government bonds in order to weaken their currency.

      % US treasuries are used as a safe and easily accessible place to park their money:
      % * Strong reputation for creditworthiness
      % * Right to tax citizens and corporations of the wealthiest country on earth
      % * Privilege of being able to print the world's reserve currency
      % * US bonds are the most liquid tradable assets on earth
      % * The FX market is US dollar centric

      % Investors also use US treasury bonds as a safe haven. During times of financial turmoil and crisis, investors bought US bonds, bringing bond yields down.
      % **Bond price and yields hold an inverse relationship**

      Following the transition to floating exchange rates in 1971, many nations were compelled to accumulate substantial foreign exchange reserves to defend their currency pegs. Additionally, central banks frequently purchase US government bonds, selling their domestic currencies in the process, to deliberately weaken their own exchange rates to support export economies. US Treasuries serve as a globally preferred, secure asset due to several distinct advantages: the unparalleled creditworthiness of the US government, its taxation authority over the world's wealthiest economy, the unique privilege of issuing the primary global reserve currency, and the unmatched liquidity of the US bond market. Consequently, during periods of financial instability, investors instinctively seek refuge in US Treasuries. This surge in demand drives bond prices upward, which concurrently forces bond yields downward, reflecting the fundamental inverse relationship between bond prices and yields.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Corporate Bond Market}

      % Why do companies borrow in the corporate bond market?
      % In most countries, a discrepancy in tax treatment means that debt repayments lower a company's tax bill by reducing pre-tax profits through interest payments.
      % Companies can borrow money for longer terms from the bond market than from banks, which are not typically keen on making long term loans to corporations.

      Corporations primarily access the bond market for debt financing due to favorable tax treatments and term structures. In most tax jurisdictions, interest payments on debt are tax-deductible, effectively reducing a firm's taxable income and lowering its overall tax burden. Furthermore, the corporate bond market provides access to long-term financing that is generally unavailable through traditional commercial banks, which tend to prefer issuing shorter-term business loans.

    \end{core}

  \subsection{Bond Mechanics}

    \begin{core}{Bond Structure}

      % Bonds are IOU notes promising to pay regular fixed amount payments (coupons) and a large payment at maturation (principal); this is fixed income.

      Fundamentally, bonds are promissory notes that obligate the issuer to make regular, fixed interest payments, known as coupons, to the holder. Upon maturation, the issuer is also required to return the initial borrowing amount, referred to as the principal. This predictable stream of cash flows is the defining characteristic of a fixed-income instrument.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Bond Yields}

      % Bond Yields are similar to APR on a credit card.
      % The main input to the calculation of yield is the price of the bond (which fluctuates).
      % Fixed income ≠ fixed yield.
      % The remaining repayments and the floating bond price are used to calculate the yield.
      % Yields make the diversity of the 2.1 million bonds in the market.

      % A bond yield is simply the equivalent interest rate on a bank account that would provide an identical end amount given that the coupon payments are deposited into this bank account upon receiving.

      % Bond yields are nothing more than advertised deposit rates; the difference is that bond yields fluctuate as the price of the bond moves.

      A bond's yield can be conceptualized as being similar to the annual percentage rate (APR) on a loan or an advertised deposit rate for a savings account. However, while the income generated by a bond is fixed, its yield is not. The primary variable in calculating a bond's yield is its current market price, which fluctuates continuously based on market conditions. Therefore, the yield represents the equivalent interest rate a theoretical bank account would need to offer to produce an identical final return, assuming all coupon payments are deposited immediately upon receipt.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Price-Yield Relationship}

      % Generally speaking, the lower the agreed purchase price for a bond, the greater the APR, or yield, as a smaller up-front sum results in the same fixed amount repayments down the road.
      % EX. Perpetual bond is a bond whose principal is never repaid → yield and price are a perfect mirror image.

      % New Lenders   | New Borrowers
      % --------------|--------------
      % High Yields   | Good / Bad
      % Low Yields    | Bad / Good

      % *Servicing a debt refers to sustaining one's existing amount of borrowing by paying coupons.

      A fundamental principle of fixed-income markets is the inverse relationship between a bond's purchase price and its yield. If an investor pays a lower up-front price for a set of fixed future cash flows, the effective rate of return, or yield, naturally increases. This dynamic is perfectly illustrated by a perpetual bond—an instrument whose principal is never repaid—where price and yield operate as exact mirror images. Consequently, high market yields are advantageous for prospective lenders but detrimental to new borrowers, whereas low yields benefit new borrowers at the expense of lenders. In this context, servicing debt simply refers to the ongoing cost of maintaining existing borrowing obligations through regular coupon payments.

    \end{core}

\section{Valuation Framework}

  \subsection{Credit Analysis}

    \begin{core}{Credit Overview}

      % Bond prices today reflect beliefs on repayment likelihood tomorrow. The assumption when calculating yield, however, is that the income is truly fixed, that the full repayment amounts promised will materialize. Therefore, notwithstanding your fears about the solvency of the borrower, when it comes to calculating the yield, you assume that you will get the same cash for a lower price.

      % When bond investors (lenders) begin to doubt the creditworthiness of a borrower, yields move sharply upwards.

      % If the market believes that the government may have trouble paying money back, the price of the bond will be lower and, therefore, the yield higher. This makes future borrowing more expensive for this government. In this way, the bond market instills fiscal discipline in governments.

      Current bond prices fundamentally reflect market expectations regarding the likelihood of future repayment. However, the standard mathematical calculation of yield assumes that the promised cash flows will materialize completely. Therefore, when market participants begin to doubt a borrower's solvency, they demand a lower initial price for the bond to compensate for the elevated risk. Because the nominal cash flows remain unchanged, this drop in price mathematically results in a sharply higher yield. For sovereign debt, if investors suspect a government may struggle to meet its obligations, the resulting decline in bond prices and subsequent spike in yields significantly increases the cost of future borrowing. Through this mechanism, the bond market actively imposes fiscal discipline on sovereign nations.

    \end{core} 

    \begin{core}{Sovereign Credit Risk Metrics}

      % To keep tabs on a government's creditworthiness, investors commonly look at three metrics:

      % 1. Debt/GDP
      
        % As a rule of thumb, the higher a government's debt burden in proportion to its GDP, the riskier its bonds. For higher risk, investors will demand a higher return in the form of an elevated yield. There is, however, no general rule on when bond yields will spike. Japan is one of the most heavily indebted nations in the world, yet it has one of the lowest government bond yields.

        % When debt is repaid, this payment does not include itself in GDP; the original spending of money brought from this debt is when GDP increases.

      % 2. Deficit/GDP

        % Negative deficits means that the governments are spending more money than they are bringing in through taxation, relying on the bond market to finance their deficit.
        % The higher the deficit, the higher the interest payments go up as a proportion of the government budget.
        % Again, there is no hard/fast rule on the % deficit that will make investors bail.

      % 3. Repayment Schedule (Rolling Over Debt)

        % Borrowing over the short term attracts a lower annual interest rate because it lowers risk for the lender. This is tempting because it lowers interest payments.
        % EX. governments may theoretically repay debt by issuing new debt (rolling over debt).
        % The downside of taking advantage of low interest short-term debt is that lenders can pull the rug and suddenly demand very high yields.
        % By borrowing in the short-term, and refinancing the debt on a regular basis, the US government takes advantage of the lower interest rates available for short-term borrowers. US isn't afraid of investors pulling the rug because of its strong creditworthiness.

      To assess sovereign creditworthiness, investors primarily evaluate three macroeconomic metrics. First, the Debt-to-GDP ratio serves as a general indicator of leverage; typically, a higher proportional debt burden implies greater default risk, prompting investors to demand elevated yields. However, this is not an absolute rule, as evidenced by Japan, which maintains exceptionally low borrowing costs despite holding one of the highest debt burdens globally. Second, the Deficit-to-GDP ratio highlights the extent to which a government's expenditures exceed its tax revenues. Persistent deficits require continuous financing through the bond market, subsequently increasing the proportion of the national budget consumed by debt interest payments. Finally, a nation's repayment schedule, or its strategy for rolling over debt, is scrutinized. While issuing short-term debt is attractive due to lower associated interest rates, it exposes the borrower to refinancing risk—the danger that lenders may suddenly demand significantly higher yields. Due to its robust credit profile, the US government is uniquely positioned to exploit the cost advantages of short-term borrowing and continuous refinancing without substantial fear of abrupt lender withdrawal.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Credit Risk Indicators}

      % Credit Ratings
      % Ratings agencies give bonds a rating ranging from AAA to D.

      % With corporate bonds, ratings higher than BBB- or at BBB- are called "investment grade"; BB+ and below are called "non-investment grade" AKA "junk bonds".

      % Keep in mind that the track record for credit rating agencies is decidedly patchier for corporate bonds that it is for government bonds.

      % Credit Default Swaps (CDSs)
      % CDSs are like insurance on bonds going bust.

      % CDSs often provide a more timely warning of impending default than ratings. This is b/c they are "real-time readings based on traded instruments. The higher the CDS spread, the higher the risk.

      Market participants rely on several formal and market-implied indicators to gauge credit risk. Credit rating agencies assign letter grades to bond issues, ranging from AAA down to D. Within the corporate sector, bonds rated BBB- and above are classified as "investment grade," whereas those rated BB+ and below are categorized as "non-investment grade" or colloquially as "junk bonds." It is widely noted that the historical performance of these agencies has been less reliable for corporate issuances compared to sovereign debt. Alternatively, Credit Default Swaps (CDS) function as a form of market-traded insurance against bond defaults. Because they are actively traded instruments, CDS spreads provide a real-time assessment of credit risk that frequently serves as a more timely early warning indicator than static agency ratings. Simply put, a wider CDS spread denotes higher perceived market risk.

    \end{core}

  \subsection{Macroeconomic Drivers}

    \begin{core}{Macroeconomic Drivers}

      % Overview
      % ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
      % The other major driver of bond valuation is the macroeconomic environment:
      % * Short-Term Interest Rate
      % * Inflation

      % Short-Term Interest Rates
      % ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
      % Short-term interest rates directly affect the yields of the bond market.

      % Non-US Government treasury bonds must always check their prices/yield with US treasury bonds; The US is the most creditworthy, thus all other bonds w/ the same details as US bonds must always be cheaper (thus higher yield) b/c of the added risk.

      % This yield competition phenomenon is simply price competition masked by the jargon of the bond market.

      % Inflation
      % ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
      % Inflation will erode the purchasing power of a bond's coupon and principal payments, thus with higher inflation, bond yields increase as people demand lower prices for the same nominal returns.
      % Bond yields are called nominal and not real. You do not adjust the yield calculation for inflation.

      % Summary
      % ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
      % * As repayments to bondholders are fixed, inflation will corrode the purchasing power of fixed bond repayments, sending yields up.
      % * As US government bond yields serve as benchmarks for all investments, yields on other bonds tend to move with them.

      Beyond creditworthiness, bond valuations are profoundly influenced by macroeconomic factors, specifically short-term interest rates and inflation expectations. Short-term interest rates exert a direct and immediate effect on broader market yields. In the sovereign space, non-US government bonds invariably price themselves relative to US Treasuries. Because the United States is generally perceived as the most creditworthy sovereign borrower, bonds issued by other nations must offer a lower price—and consequently a higher yield—to compensate investors for the additional inherent risk. This dynamic is essentially basic price competition expressed through the terminology of yield. Furthermore, inflation is a critical driver of bond markets. Because bond payments are fixed, inflation steadily erodes the future purchasing power of both coupon and principal disbursements. In response to rising inflation, investors demand lower initial purchase prices for the same nominal cash flows, driving yields upward. It is crucial to recognize that quoted bond yields are nominal figures and do not inherently adjust for inflation. Ultimately, because US government yields serve as the foundational benchmark for global investments, yield movements across international developed markets tend to be highly correlated.

    \end{core}

\section{Monetary Policy \& Market Dynamics}

  \subsection{Central Banking}

    % How do governments set short-term bond yields, that is, how they attempt to nudge the economy towards slow, steady growth?

    \begin{core}{Central Bank Mandates}

      % Inflation is psychological and is most prevalent during wartime when governments print large amounts of money to fund the war.
      % Delation is also psychological and bad for the economy because consumers keep delaying purchase in response to constant price decreases.

      % Bond markets are ravaged by inflation which became a major issue for economies in the 1970s.

      % *High inflation will erode the price of bonds and send yields up.

      Central banks actively intervene in money markets to steer economies toward stable, sustainable growth, managing two primary psychological extremes: inflation and deflation. Inflation, which often historically spikes during wartime due to excessive monetary expansion, severely erodes the real value of fixed-income assets, as notably witnessed during the economic turbulence of the 1970s. Conversely, deflation is equally destructive, as anticipating consistently falling prices incentivizes consumers to indefinitely delay spending, which stalls economic activity. Central banks must therefore carefully balance these forces, operating with the understanding that unchecked inflation will inevitably drive bond prices down and corresponding yields up.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Inflation Measures}

      % Central Banks use three main statistics to gauge the economies' inflation:
      % * GDP deflator
      % * CPI stat
      % * PCE stat (PCE strips out volatile costs of food and energy, allowing for a more trustworthy representation of consumer consumption)

      % However since these statistics only look at the past and CPI forecasts are only so accurate, investors usually look at TIPS to gauge inflation.

      To assess current inflationary pressures, central banks rely on three primary statistical metrics: the GDP deflator, the Consumer Price Index (CPI), and the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index. The PCE is particularly favored by policymakers because it isolates the core inflation trend by stripping out highly volatile food and energy costs. However, because these indices are inherently backward-looking indicators, bond market investors primarily look to specific traded financial instruments to gauge real-time, forward-looking inflation expectations.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Treasury Inflation Proteced Securities (TIPS)}

      % Treasury Inflation Protected Securities (TIPS)
      % ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
      % Instead of having "fixed-income" payments to the lender, TIPS compensate the lender in the event of inflation, using CPI as a guide.
      % By comparing the price of TIPS and non-inflation protected bonds, investors can glean inflation expectations. Higher inflation → higher demand for TIPS relative to normal bonds → greater difference in yields.

      Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) are explicitly designed to shield lenders from purchasing power erosion by adjusting their principal and coupon payments in accordance with changes in the CPI. By analyzing the pricing differential between TIPS and standard, non-protected Treasury bonds of the exact same maturity, market participants can precisely extract the market's implied inflation expectations. When inflation forecasts rise, investor demand for TIPS increases relative to standard bonds, causing the yield spread between the two assets to widen.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Output Gap}

      % Output Gap
      % ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
      % The output gap is the difference between an economy's potential output and its actual output.
      % When the economy is tight—the actual output is higher than the potential—it is a frequent sign of inflation, while slackness frequently coincides with deflation.

      % Output Gap% = (actual output $B - potential output $B) / potential output $B

      % Once the central bankers have pored over all manner of inflation statistics, examined the output gap, and studied other data sources, they decide whether to cool the porridge, heat the porridge, or do nothing.

      The output gap represents the macroeconomic difference between an economy's actual production and its theoretical potential output. When an economy operates "tightly"—meaning actual output exceeds potential capacity—it frequently signals impending inflation. Conversely, economic "slack" generally coincides with deflationary pressures. Central bankers synthesize data on the output gap alongside traditional inflation metrics to formulate monetary policy, determining whether it is necessary to cool an overheating economy, stimulate a stagnant one, or maintain current interest rates.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Policy Toolkit}

      % Policy Toolkit
      % ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
      % Short-Term Interest Rates
      % When short-term interest rates go up, it becomes more attractive to deposit cash, dissuading consumption/investment, lowering the actual output, and bring inflation down (which coincides w/ tight output).
      % The short term interest rate decision set by the Federal Open Market Committee 8 times a year are tremendous news as the US bond yields are influenced by it.

      % Forward Guidance & Statements
      % Small difference in past and recent FOMC statements can hint at a shift in bond yield indirectly.

      % Short-term interest rate estimates:
      % The futures and options market allows us to determine the probability of certain future FOMC decisions.

      The primary mechanism central banks use to influence the economy is the adjustment of short-term interest rates. By raising these rates, central banks make holding cash deposits more attractive, which systematically dissuades consumer borrowing and corporate investment. This reduction in aggregate demand lowers actual economic output and effectively suppresses inflation. In the United States, the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) determines this pivotal rate eight times a year, generating immense market impact as it directly calibrates US bond yields. Beyond immediate rate changes, the FOMC heavily relies on "forward guidance"—carefully crafted policy statements where minute linguistic shifts signal future interest rate trajectories. Market participants actively price these expectations into the futures and options markets, allowing analysts to derive clear probabilities regarding upcoming monetary policy decisions.

    \end{core}

  \subsection{The Yield Curve}

    \begin{core}{Yield Curve}

      % The yield curve for most countries show a positive sloping curve with higher yields given the longer term bonds due to the higher risk added with greater chance of inaccuracy in inflation predictions.

      % The difference between the yield on the longer maturity bonds and shorter-maturity bonds is called the "term premium".

      % The yield curve represents the cost of borrowing and, therefore, it reverberates throughout the markets and the world economy.
      % When the Fed alters the left-hand end of the yield curve—short-term interest rate—it affects prices and yields of the whole $101T bond market.

      % * The yield curve represents the cost of borrowing for various loan lengths.
      % * Yield curves are naturally upward sloping due to elevated risk of long-term lending.
      % * Corporate bonds are priced using a spread off the government yield curve so the yield curve indirectly regulates company funding.
      % * Consumer borrowing for big ticket items is priced off the yield curve.
      % * Yields of developed economics are correlated, which means that the overall movement in yields has a global impact.
      % * The far left of the yield curve is the overnight interest rate set by the central bank.
      % * The far right of the yield curve is driven primarily by inflation expectations.
      % * While the left-hand end of the yield curve is locked, the right hand end float freely.
      % * A steep yield curve signals improving times.
      % * A flat yield curve signals worsening times.
      % * An inverted yield curve often precedes a recession.

      The yield curve visually represents the cost of borrowing across various maturation horizons and serves as a fundamental barometer for the global economy. Under normal conditions, the curve slopes upward, reflecting the "term premium"—the additional yield demanded by investors to compensate for the elevated risks and inflation uncertainties inherent in locking up capital for extended periods. The far-left segment of the curve is rigidly tethered to the overnight interest rate established by the central bank. Consequently, when the Federal Reserve adjusts this rate, the effects cascade across the entire \$101 trillion fixed-income market. In contrast, the far-right segment of the curve floats freely, driven predominantly by aggregate market inflation expectations. Because corporate debt and consumer loans are priced as a spread above the sovereign yield curve, its movements dictate global borrowing costs. Furthermore, the curve's shape provides vital economic signaling: a steepening curve typically indicates anticipation of robust economic growth, a flattening curve suggests deteriorating conditions, and an inverted yield curve is historically recognized as a reliable precursor to an economic recession.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Market Impacts}

      % Corporate Impact

        % Almost all corporate investments are multiyear, thus medium or long-term borrowing is used to match the project's duration.
        % Facing the same risks of short term interest rate hikes and inflation (both of which will dent bond prices for the lender), corporations must also face the fact that they are typically less creditworthy than governments, thus they must typically provide higher yields for the bonds of the same maturity.
        % This difference in yield between the treasury bonds and corporate bonds is called "the spread".
        % Corporate yield curves typically move in cohesion with government yield curve increases and decreases. Thus the spread stays relatively the same although overall yields either spiked or hiked (governments use this as a regulator for economic growth and inflation).
        % The gap between all US government 10-year bond yield and all corporate investment-grade 10 year bond yield is called the "corporate spread".
        % * When the corporate spread increases (widening, cheapening, steepening)
        % * When the corporate spread decreases (tightening, richening, flattening)

      % Consumer Impact

        % US 30-year mortgage rates are priced off of government 10 year bond yields as the spread between these figures are typically consistent.
        % Government bond yields therefore influence the affordability of homes and activity in the housing market (a large GDP portion).

      % Global Impact

        % 10 year bond yields of several advanced economies are strongly correlated, thus they are stimulated/depressed in sync.

      The dynamics of the government yield curve exert profound impacts across all sectors of the economy. In the corporate sector, firms typically match the duration of their liabilities with their multi-year capital investments by issuing medium to long-term bonds. Because corporations possess inherently lower creditworthiness than sovereign issuers, their debt commands a higher yield. The structural difference between a corporate bond's yield and a maturity-matched government bond is known as the "spread." As central banks manipulate the foundational government yield curve, corporate yields generally move in tandem, maintaining relatively stable spreads. However, fluctuations in the specific "corporate spread"—the yield gap between a 10-year US Treasury and a 10-year investment-grade corporate bond—reflect shifting market risk appetite, with expansions termed "widening" or "steepening" and contractions termed "tightening" or "flattening." These market dynamics directly affect consumers, as key lending instruments like the 30-year US mortgage rate are tightly anchored to 10-year government bond yields, heavily influencing broader housing market activity. Additionally, due to the highly integrated nature of global finance, benchmark 10-year sovereign yields across advanced economies remain strongly correlated, moving synchronously in response to overarching macroeconomic trends.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Yield Curve Dynamics}

      % The Yield Curve in Motion
      % US government bond yields and, therefore, prices are principally driven by short-term interest rates and inflation. Creditworthiness is a non-issue, as we saw. Investors can therefore make profitable trades by correctly predicting the Fed's next move and inflation.
      % The left hand end of the yield curve is set by the Fed's short-term interest rate decisions. It moves when the Fed changes the rate at the FOMC meeting.

      % The Fed funds target rate moves in tandem with the left-hand end of the yield curve.
      % The left hand end of the yield curve is simply the Fed Funds Target Rate.

      % The right hand end of the yield curve is influenced majorly by ingrained inflation expectations.

      % When inflation rises, the right hand side of the yield curve is free to move through the natural supply and demand of bond investors while the left hand side is tethered to the Fed Fund Target Rate.

      The structural dynamics of the US Treasury yield curve are principally driven by short-term interest rates and forward-looking inflation expectations, as sovereign credit risk is effectively considered negligible. This environment allows astute investors to position themselves based on forecasts of the Federal Reserve's policy shifts and broader inflationary trends. The left-hand anchor of the yield curve acts in lockstep with the Federal Funds Target Rate, shifting discretely based on formal FOMC decisions. Conversely, the right-hand tail of the curve is unanchored, freely oscillating according to the natural supply and demand forces exerted by bond investors reacting to ingrained inflation expectations. Therefore, as inflation forecasts shift, the long end of the curve actively re-prices while the short end remains strictly tethered to prevailing central bank policy.

    \end{core}

    \begin{core}{Economic Signals}

      % 2003 Dotcom bust recovery
      % The Fed might need to rein in inflation and preemptively lift short-term interest rates. With expected hikes, bond investors did not want to lose money by holding bonds (raised long-term yields resulted). Steep yield curves are a sign of an accelerating economy.
      % Short-term interest rates finally rose and investors aggressively bought long-term bonds in expectation of a slowing economy and currently high rates.
      % This drove long-term bond yields down resulting in an inverted yield curve a common precursor to an economic downturn.

      The predictive power of the yield curve is well-documented in modern economic history, such as during the recovery phase following the early 2000s dot-com contraction. As economic activity initially accelerated, market participants anticipated that the Federal Reserve would preemptively hike short-term interest rates to curb emerging inflation. Fearing that rising rates would erode bond values, investors aggressively sold off long-dated bonds, driving long-term yields higher and creating a steeply sloping yield curve—a classic hallmark of an accelerating economy. Subsequently, as the central bank actually executed these short-term rate hikes, the economic outlook shifted. Anticipating that higher borrowing costs would inevitably constrain growth and cool the economy, investors rushed back into the safety of long-term bonds to lock in the elevated yields. This surge in demand drove long-term yields back down, ultimately forcing long-term rates below short-term rates. This phenomenon resulted in an inverted yield curve, serving as a quintessential leading indicator for an impending economic downturn.

    \end{core}
